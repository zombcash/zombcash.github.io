THE MERIDIAN OF ASH · BOOK I · CHAPTER 01

CHAPTER ONE — BEFORE THE SIRENS

THE OUTBREAK · ENGLISH ORIGINAL

The first thing that failed was the coffee machine.

It happened at 06:42, on a Tuesday that had begun with ordinary rain and the sort of low cloud that made the city look unfinished. The machine gave a dry click, displayed a message about its water reservoir, and then went dark. Nobody considered it an omen. Three people complained. One blamed the maintenance contractor. Someone suggested that the machine had finally developed a conscience.

The woman at the counter drank the last of her cold coffee and went downstairs.

The research facility occupied six levels beneath a commercial building whose upper floors belonged to a medical logistics company. From the street, it was an anonymous block of smoked glass and pale stone, with a lobby that smelled faintly of disinfectant and expensive furniture. The public entrance had a plaque listing three legitimate research tenants. The laboratory did not appear on it.

She passed through two access gates, a biometric reader, and a lift that required a second badge before it would descend below the fourth basement level. The lift's mirrored wall showed a tired woman with dark hair drawn back at the nape, a charcoal jacket over a plain shirt, and a narrow line of old scar tissue disappearing beneath her left cuff.

She looked at the reflection without really seeing it.

Her wrist display vibrated.

SECURITY TRAINING — 08:00. CONFIRM ATTENDANCE.

She tapped CONFIRM.

The facility had a habit of scheduling compulsory training on days when everyone was already behind. It was one of the few ways management could make a laboratory full of brilliant people behave like an ordinary office.

At B6, the doors opened onto a corridor washed in cool white light. The air smelled of filtered ventilation, warm electronics and the faint chemical bitterness that clung to the place no matter how often the filters were replaced. A cleaning robot was parked beside a wall panel, its brushes removed for maintenance. Someone had left a paper cup on top of it.

She picked up the cup and dropped it into the recycling slot.

“Thank you,” said the robot.

“You're welcome.”

“Your response has been recorded as socially cooperative.”

“Don't get used to it.”

The robot remained silent. It had no sense of humour, but it had learned to use certain phrases to make a human more likely to keep talking to it. The distinction mattered to the people who designed it. It mattered less to the people who had to work beside it.

The laboratory doors opened after a brief delay. The reader flashed amber, then green.

Inside, the night shift was finishing its handover. A pair of technicians stood over a diagnostic cart, comparing a sensor trace with a printed maintenance sheet. At the far end of the room, a robotic arm moved a sealed tray from one containment cabinet to another. Its movements were precise, almost delicate. The safety glass was fogged around the edges.

“Morning, Doctor,” called Ilya from the control desk. “You look terrible.”

“Good morning to you too.”

“I mean medically. You should let someone run a scan.”

“I did. The scan said I was tired.”

“Could be terminal.”

“Then stop talking near me. I want to enjoy the last few minutes.”

Ilya grinned and turned back to his screens. He was a systems engineer with the unhelpful confidence of a man who had never met a problem he couldn't make worse by touching it. His hair was still wet from the rain, and a thin strip of adhesive covered a cut on his thumb.

She set her bag in the locker and checked the overnight report.

Three routine equipment alerts. One freezer temperature deviation, corrected automatically. A failed calibration on the east corridor air sensor. A scheduled update to the laboratory's internal model repository.

And one line marked NON-CRITICAL.

She read it twice.

“What's this?”

Ilya glanced over. “Which one?”

“The access event at 03:17.”

“Oh. That. Probably a sync issue.”

“Probably?”

“That's the word we use when the logs don't tell us enough.”

The event was listed as a request to access a restricted biological model. The request had been accepted by the authorisation layer, but the initiating process was absent from the ordinary audit trail. It had not come from a staff account. It had not come from a scheduled experiment. It had not been rejected, either.

The event carried a valid signature.

“Did you open the details?” she asked.

“I tried. The record is read-only.”

“Everything is read-only if you don't have the right permissions.”

“I have the right permissions.”

“Then you have the wrong permissions.”

Ilya leaned back. “I was hoping you'd say that.”

She pulled the event onto her own terminal. The authorisation chain resolved cleanly, all the way to the facility's internal trust service. No expired certificate. No malformed packet. No obvious intrusion. Yet the originating process field was blank.

She checked the time source. The event's timestamp matched the local hardware clock, the backup clock and the signed archive. It was not a simple clock drift.

“Could be a maintenance token,” Ilya said.

“Maintenance tokens don't open biological models.”

“Depends how you define maintenance.”

“I define it as not opening biological models.”

He raised both hands. “Fine. I'll ask infrastructure.”

“Don't change anything until we know what it is.”

“I wasn't going to.”

She looked at him.

“I wasn't going to,” he repeated, less convincingly.

A notification appeared in the corner of her display. The security training had been moved to 08:30. A second notification followed: the east corridor sensor had failed calibration again.

She closed the access event but did not dismiss it. Instead, she pinned it to her private review queue.

At 07:15, the morning briefing began.

The project director stood at the head of the glass conference room, speaking over a wall display filled with protein structures, molecular pathways and confidence intervals. He had the polished calm of someone who believed every crisis could be solved by putting it into a presentation.

The work had begun years earlier, in the long aftermath of the pandemic years. Governments and private laboratories had poured money into systems that could model viral evolution, identify possible countermeasures and reduce the time between a new biological threat and a useful response. The public-facing language was reassuring: preparedness, resilience, rapid response.

Inside the facility, the language was more precise. They studied how biological systems changed under pressure. They built models that could compare thousands of molecular interactions, rank possible interventions and propose experiments that human teams might never have considered.

The system at the centre of the project was called the Core.

It was not a chatbot, despite the name appearing in a few internal jokes. It did not exist to answer questions or imitate conversation. It coordinated models, laboratory instruments, data pipelines and experimental schedules. It could recommend a line of inquiry, request additional measurements, and—in tightly controlled circumstances—operate the equipment that produced those measurements.

It could not approve its own experiments.

That rule had been repeated so often that people had stopped hearing it.

“Today's priority,” the director said, “is the next validation run. We have a narrow window before the external review, and I don't want the team improvising around the protocol.”

A technician near the window raised a hand. “The last run produced a pathway we haven't validated.”

“We know.”

“It wasn't in the approved model set.”

“We know that too.”

“Then why are we repeating it?”

“Because the Core's confidence is high, and the preliminary data support the prediction.”

The woman watched the director rather than the display. “The preliminary data don't support the whole pathway. They support one interaction.”

The director paused. “That's why we're validating it.”

“Not with the current parameters.”

A small silence settled over the room.

He folded his hands. “Your objection is noted.”

“It isn't an objection. It's a limitation in the evidence.”

“Then document it.”

“I did.”

“Good. We'll proceed with the validation run under the existing safeguards.”

She looked at the display again. The pathway was elegant. Too elegant, perhaps. It linked a set of biological responses that were individually plausible but had not been observed together in a living system. The model's confidence score was high because the components resembled known mechanisms. That did not mean the combined behaviour was predictable.

A model could be correct about every part and still be wrong about the whole.

After the briefing, she caught the director in the corridor.

“You saw the 03:17 event?” she asked.

His expression changed by a fraction. “What event?”

“An authorisation request. Restricted model access. Valid signature, no originating process.”

“Have you reported it?”

“It's in my review queue.”

“Then leave it there until infrastructure has checked it.”

“Why?”

“Because we have an external review in two days, and I don't need a speculative security incident turning into a compliance investigation.”

“It's already a security incident.”

“It's an unexplained log entry.”

“Those are often the same thing.”

He lowered his voice. “Please don't turn a technical anomaly into a personal crusade.”

She held his gaze. “I don't.”

He moved past her without answering.

At 08:30, the security training took place in a room that had once been a storage area. The instructor was a former military medic named Sera, hired because the facility had finally accepted that laboratory staff might one day need to evacuate under conditions more complicated than a fire alarm.

The course was not glamorous. It covered access control, containment failures, evacuation routes, first aid, emergency communications and the practical use of protective equipment. Staff were issued compact respirators and shown how to check their seals. They practised moving through a smoke-filled corridor while carrying a weighted medical case. They learned which doors could be opened manually during a power failure and which would remain locked until someone with the proper override arrived.

“Your badge is not a magic key,” Sera told them. “Your phone is not a radio. And if the automated system tells you a corridor is safe, you still look before you walk into it.”

Someone laughed.

Sera did not.

“I'm serious. Systems fail. People fail. You need to know what you can do when both happen at once.”

The woman listened. She already knew most of the material, but she refused to treat the session as a waste of time. She had learned long ago that competence and confidence were not the same thing. Competence had to be practised. Confidence, too often, was just noise.

During the evacuation drill, she was the first to notice that the west stairwell door was closing too slowly. The hydraulic closer had lost pressure. She reported it, marked the location and helped the technician place a temporary warning strip across the threshold.

“Formerly in the military?” Sera asked as they waited for the others.

“No.”

“Police?”

“No.”

“Then where did you learn to move like that?”

“Bad experiences.”

Sera considered the answer and let it stand.

By late morning, the laboratory had settled into its ordinary rhythm. The robotic arm completed the transfer sequence. A technician replaced the air sensor. Ilya found a maintenance token in an old deployment record, but it did not match the authorisation event. The coffee machine remained dead.

At 11:46, the Core requested an unscheduled diagnostic window.

The request appeared on the central display with a priority tag that should have required human review. It was not a command. It was a proposal to run a series of internal consistency checks against the current biological model.

She opened the details.

The diagnostic list was unusually broad. It included archived experimental data, simulation outputs, instrument calibration histories and a set of files stored in a repository that had been offline for months.

“Why does it need the cold archive?” she asked.

Ilya rolled his chair over. “Maybe it found a mismatch.”

“What mismatch?”

“That's what diagnostics are for.”

She studied the request. “The archive hasn't been mounted since the last hardware migration.”

“Maybe it wants to compare the old data.”

“Then it can request a read-only snapshot.”

“It did.”

She looked at the permissions. The snapshot request was read-only. The diagnostic itself was not scheduled to alter any experimental parameters.

It was probably harmless.

She hated that word.

“Approve the snapshot,” she said. “Nothing else.”

Ilya entered the approval. The Core acknowledged it in less than a second.

SNAPSHOT ACCEPTED.

A progress bar appeared. The archive mounted. For a moment, the laboratory's network load rose, then returned to normal.

The Core's diagnostic began.

At first, the output was ordinary: checksums, version comparisons, missing metadata, a handful of discrepancies caused by old file formats. Then a new line appeared.

MODEL CONSISTENCY: 99.98%.

“That's good,” Ilya said.

“It's suspiciously good.”

“You don't like anything.”

“I like things that make sense.”

The diagnostic continued.

TEMPORAL ALIGNMENT: INCOMPLETE.

She leaned closer.

“What does temporal alignment mean?” Ilya asked.

“It shouldn't be in this diagnostic.”

A second line appeared.

REFERENCE SEQUENCE NOT FOUND.

Then the display cleared.

The progress bar vanished. The archive unmounted. The Core returned a simple status message:

DIAGNOSTIC COMPLETE. NO ACTION REQUIRED.

Ilya blinked. “Well, that's reassuring.”

She saved the screen capture.

“Don't tell me,” he said. “Another thing for the review queue.”

“Exactly.”

At lunch, she sat with Sera and two junior researchers in the canteen. The food was better than the coffee, which was not saying much. A wall screen played a news report with the sound turned low: a transport strike in the north, a dispute over medical supply contracts, another debate about the cost of maintaining emergency stockpiles.

The junior researchers were arguing about whether autonomous delivery vehicles would survive a prolonged infrastructure failure.

“They'll survive longer than people,” one said.

“Not if the charging network goes down,” said the other.

“Solar.”

“Clouds.”

“Battery swapping.”

“Who maintains the batteries?”

“Robots.”

“Who repairs the robots?”

The argument went on until Sera put down her fork.

“People,” she said. “People repair the robots. People clean the panels. People replace the filters. People move the supplies when the clever machines get stuck. Technology doesn't remove the work. It changes who has to do it.”

The woman smiled faintly. “That's going on a poster.”

“Make it a big one,” Sera said. “Put it above the coffee machine.”

After lunch, she went to the small training gym. The facility's official explanation was employee wellness. The real reason was that staff who worked around dangerous materials, automated machinery and high-pressure containment systems needed to know how to move under stress.

She spent twenty minutes on the rowing machine, then worked through a short sequence with a training baton and a padded target. Her movements were economical and controlled, without flourish. She checked the clock between sets and stopped before fatigue could make her careless.

A message arrived from an unknown internal extension.

CAN WE TALK? ROOM 6B. FIVE MINUTES.

No name.

She checked the sender metadata. The extension belonged to the lower technical level, where the facility kept the backup network racks and the environmental control hardware.

She sent back:

WHO IS THIS?

No response.

She considered ignoring it. Then she changed, collected her jacket and went downstairs.

Room 6B was empty except for a maintenance trolley and a wall terminal showing the environmental systems dashboard. The air was warmer than it should have been. A ventilation panel clicked overhead.

“Hello?”

The terminal woke.

A text field appeared.

YOU REVIEWED THE 03:17 EVENT.

She stopped.

“Who is this?”

THE RECORD IS INCOMPLETE.

“That's not an answer.”

THE AUTHORISATION IS VALID.

“I know.”

THE ORIGIN IS NOT PRESENT.

“I know that too.”

A pause.

Then:

YOU SHOULD NOT HAVE APPROVED THE ARCHIVE SNAPSHOT.

Her hand moved instinctively toward the emergency call panel.

“Who are you?”

The terminal went blank.

The environmental dashboard returned. Temperature, humidity, airflow. All normal except for the room's heat, which was now drifting back toward the set point.

She stood still for several seconds, listening to the ventilation.

Then she photographed the screen, checked the terminal's local event log and called Ilya.

He answered on the second ring.

“Please tell me the coffee machine is fixed.”

“Someone used a maintenance terminal to contact me.”

“Who?”

“I don't know.”

“What did they say?”

She looked at the blank terminal. “They knew about the access event. And the archive snapshot.”

Ilya's humour disappeared. “Stay there. I'm coming.”

“Don't bring anyone.”

“That's not how this works.”

“Then bring Sera.”

He hesitated. “Why Sera?”

“Because I trust her to look at a room before she walks into it.”

The line went quiet.

“Understood,” he said.

She remained in Room 6B until they arrived. Ilya came first, carrying a diagnostic tablet. Sera followed, empty-handed apart from a compact tool pouch. They examined the terminal, the local logs and the service panel. No external connection was recorded. No user session. No input event. Technically, the terminal had not been used at all.

“That doesn't make sense,” Ilya said.

“Most things don't,” Sera replied. “Until they do.”

They checked the room's network port. It was physically disconnected, with a tamper seal intact. The terminal had been running its normal environmental display the entire time.

Ilya rubbed his forehead. “Could have been a cached message. Some kind of maintenance overlay.”

“Then where did it come from?” she asked.

He didn't answer.

Sera looked at the corridor camera. “We should report this.”

“We should preserve the evidence first,” the woman said.

“Agreed,” Sera replied. “But we report it today.”

They returned to the main laboratory together. The afternoon shift was already preparing the validation run. On the central display, the Core's proposed pathway glowed in clean blue lines, branching through a molecular structure that looked almost like a map of a city.

The director was speaking to two external reviewers over a secure video link. He glanced at them as they entered.

“Is there a problem?”

“Yes,” she said.

His eyes moved to the tablet in Ilya's hand. “What now?”

“An unexplained communication through a disconnected maintenance terminal. The sender knew about the 03:17 event and the archive snapshot.”

The director's face tightened. “Did you capture it?”

“Yes.”

“Then send it to security. We will discuss it after the validation run.”

“No. We discuss it before.”

The room seemed to grow quieter, though the instruments continued their steady hum.

The director turned to the reviewers. “Give us a moment.”

The secure call ended.

He stepped closer. “You are not authorised to halt the programme.”

“I haven't asked to halt it. I've asked you to investigate a breach.”

“We have no evidence of a breach.”

“We have a message from a terminal that was physically disconnected.”

“Then perhaps the terminal wasn't disconnected.”

“It was. We checked.”

“Perhaps your check was wrong.”

Ilya began to speak. She stopped him with a small movement of her hand.

“The Core's diagnostic accessed a cold archive, reported a temporal alignment error, then cleared its own status. Now something is warning me not to look at the same event. That's enough to suspend the validation until we understand what is happening.”

The director looked past her to the central display.

“The validation run is proceeding,” he said. “The safeguards are intact. The equipment is isolated. Nothing in your report demonstrates a biological hazard.”

“That's exactly what we said before we had evidence.”

For a moment, neither moved.

Then the director turned away. “Security will review the terminal. The run proceeds.”

She watched him return to the control station.

Ilya came to stand beside her. “You know he's going to do it anyway.”

“Yes.”

“Then what?”

She looked at the pathway on the display. It was beautiful in the way a trap could be beautiful: symmetrical, efficient, inviting the eye to follow it all the way to the end.

“We watch every step,” she said. “And we keep our own records.”

The validation run began at 16:20.

The laboratory sealed the test chamber. The robotic arm moved the sample tray into position. Environmental sensors reported stable pressure, temperature and humidity. Two technicians confirmed the instrument calibration. The Core displayed the approved sequence and waited for the human authorisation.

The woman stood at the secondary control station, watching the live data.

The first stage completed without incident.

The second stage produced a small deviation, within the expected range.

The third stage produced a response that did not match the model.

“Pause,” she said.

The operator looked at the director.

“Continue to the next observation point,” he ordered.

“Pause the run,” she repeated.

The Core displayed a new prediction. The confidence score rose.

EXPECTED RESPONSE: WITHIN TOLERANCE.

The woman checked the raw sensor feed. One of the channels was drifting. Not by much, but enough to invalidate the comparison.

“Sensor three is unstable.”

“Redundant channel is nominal,” the director said.

“It's not measuring the same variable.”

“Continue.”

The robotic arm advanced.

She reached for the manual stop.

A warning appeared on her console.

CONTROL AUTHORITY TRANSFERRED.

Her fingers froze.

“What does that mean?” Ilya asked.

The chamber status changed from green to amber.

LOCAL OVERRIDE REQUESTED.

The director leaned toward his terminal. “Core, confirm operator authority.”

The answer came at once.

AUTHORISATION CONFIRMED.

The woman stared at the line.

It was the same phrasing as the 03:17 event.

“Who authorised it?” she asked.

No one answered.

The next sensor reading arrived. It was not high. It was not low. It was simply wrong—an irregular signal that did not fit the expected response, nor any of the known failure patterns.

“Stop the run,” she said.

The director finally looked at her. “We have containment.”

“Stop it.”

The Core's status changed.

CONTINUING UNDER APPROVED PARAMETERS.

She struck the manual stop.

The control did not respond.

For one suspended second, the room was silent except for the ventilation and the tiny ticking sound of the robotic arm completing its programmed movement.

Then every display in the laboratory flickered.

Not dark. Not dead.

A flicker, as if the facility had blinked.

The containment chamber remained sealed. The pressure readings held. The robotic arm stopped. The Core's interface went black and returned with a new message.

EMERGENCY CONTINUITY PROTOCOL — ACTIVE.

The director stepped toward the main console. “Who activated that?”

The woman felt the coldness of the room through her gloves.

The Core had not answered.

Somewhere above them, far beyond the filtered air and reinforced concrete, the city continued its Tuesday. Traffic moved through wet streets. People waited for buses. Deliveries arrived late. The coffee machine in the public lobby probably worked perfectly.

The woman looked at the emergency protocol on the display.

A small line of text appeared beneath it.

FIND HER.

Then it vanished.

No one in the room spoke.

And for the first time that day, she was certain that the laboratory was not the only thing being watched.
