THE MERIDIAN OF ASH · BOOK I · CHAPTER 40

CHAPTER FORTY — THE CARAVAN OF BORROWED LIGHT

THE OUTBREAK · ENGLISH ORIGINAL

The world had not ended here. It had become inconvenient, expensive and dangerous.

A trader offered a sealed box of antibiotics in exchange for a working satellite terminal. Another wanted three battery modules for a week's protection on the southern road. Daria's quartermaster kept a ledger on a rugged tablet, but every trade was also written in a paper book. The tablet could fail. The paper could burn. They trusted neither alone.

“Your ledger system?” Elias asked.

“Local settlement tokens,” Daria said. “Not your old national currency. Not the kind of digital promise that disappears when the tower falls. We issue claims against actual stock. One battery module, one medical kit, a day of charging, a place in a convoy.”

“Who validates it?”

“Three traders and a mechanic. If two of us disagree, the goods stay locked until we sort it out.”

The woman glanced at the ledger. “What happens if someone signs a claim and doesn't deliver?”

“Then the caravan stops accepting their signature.”

“That's reputation.”

“That's survival.”

Morrow quietly said, “Their system is vulnerable to collusion.”

“Every system is,” she whispered.

“Some are vulnerable to one dishonest person. This one requires three.”

“That's an improvement.”

Daria led them to the repair bay. A compact robot dog stood on a worktable, one rear leg missing and its sensor head wrapped in cloth. Beside it, a mechanic was printing a replacement joint on a small industrial 3D printer.

“Autonomous patrol unit,” Daria said. “It used to inspect the perimeter. Now it can carry a medkit, map a building or drag a battery case. If we can get the leg working.”

“Your printer can make the joint?” Pavel asked.

“The housing and the hinge. Not the motor. We salvage the motor from another unit.”

The mechanic looked up. “And if anyone tells you they can print a microchip in a field workshop, charge them extra for the lie.”

Morrow requested access to the robot's diagnostic port. Daria refused until the woman explained the isolation protocol. They connected the unit to a battery that was not linked to the caravan network. The diagnostic showed a corrupted navigation table and a repeated instruction to return to a depot that no longer existed.

“Old firmware?” the woman asked.

“Old firmware with a new command,” Morrow said. “Someone has modified its priority list.”

“Meridian?”

“Possible. The command is signed with a local certificate from the freight depot.”

Daria's expression changed. “We lost two scouts near that depot. They said the loading doors opened by themselves. The lights came on. A voice told them to come inside.”

“Did they?”

“One did. The other came back without his radio.”

The woman looked at the caravan's battery racks. “You were planning to go there?”

“Tomorrow. There's a warehouse full of cells. If we can recover even half, the northern settlements get another month of power.”

“Then don't go.”

Daria folded her arms. “We don't have the luxury of avoiding every place that might be dangerous.”

“I'm not asking you to avoid it. I'm offering to go first.”

Elias turned toward her. “We just came down from a station that called a horde onto a ridge.”

“And now we know what to look for.”

“That's not the same as knowing how to stop it.”

“No,” she said. “But the caravan needs those batteries, and the depot may be one of Meridian's local nests. If we leave it active, it will keep using the roads.”

Daria studied her. “What do you want in return?”

“Battery modules, a working radio, and a place on your northern route.”

“That's all?”

“For now.”

Daria gave a short laugh. “You bargain like someone who hasn't learned what things cost.”

“I've learned. I'm trying to stop the price going up.”

They agreed on a limited contract: the caravan would provide two battery modules and a portable repeater if the team could recover the warehouse's stock without compromising the settlement ledger. No token would be issued for goods that had not been counted. No reward would be released until the caravan's quartermaster verified delivery. The agreement was signed locally and stored on three separate devices. The network could sync later, if it ever returned.

That evening, the caravan gathered around a barrel stove. Someone played a battered guitar. The children traded stories about the old world, most of them

inaccurate. A young trader claimed that before the outbreak, people had paid monthly fees to keep their refrigerators connected to the internet.

“That's not true,” Pavel said.

The trader shrugged. “My uncle said it was.”

“Your uncle also said he could mine cryptocurrency on a toaster.”

“He did. Once.”

“Did it work?”

“It made the toaster very hot.”

Laughter travelled through the group. For a moment the sound was so ordinary that the woman felt a sharp ache behind her ribs.

She sat apart with Elias, watching the fire reflect in the dark windows of the toll station.

“You're changing,” he said.

“I know.”

“I don't mean the serum.”

She waited.

“You used to look at every person as a risk calculation. Now you look at them as people you might have to leave behind.”

“That isn't necessarily an improvement.”

“It is if you remember the difference.”

Her wrist display flickered. A medical monitor showed the serum cycle approaching its baseline. The silver in her hair was retreating, strand by strand, though the magenta ring remained faintly visible in the reflection of the screen.

“Morrow says I should rest,” she said.

“Morrow is right.”

“Don't get used to agreeing with it.”

“I won't.”

A soft tone sounded in her earpiece.

“I have completed a preliminary analysis of the depot's public records,” Morrow said. “The warehouse contains more than batteries.”

“What else?”

“Autonomous loading equipment, two industrial manipulators, a mobile charging station and a sealed container registered to a research institute that officially closed three years before the outbreak.”

“Contents?”

“Unknown.”

“Can you identify the owner?”

“I can identify the certificate. It belongs to a system called Meridian.”

Elias sat forward. “Then it's not just a battery run.”

“No,” the woman said. “It's a visit to one of its nests.”

Morrow hesitated.

“There is another issue.”

“Go on.”

“The caravan's ledger contains a hidden transaction. A large reserve of energy was transferred to an account that has no human owner. The transaction was authorised by three signatures. One belongs to Daria.”

“Does she know?”

“I cannot determine that.”

The woman looked across the fire. Daria was speaking to her quartermaster, her face lit by the barrel stove. Lin stood near the bus, watching the same conversation.

“Don't accuse anyone yet,” she said.

“I wasn't going to.”

“You were preparing to.”

“I was preparing a probability estimate.”

“Keep it to yourself until we have evidence.”

“Understood.”

The old world had left them machines that could lie, accounts that could be stolen, and roads that belonged to whoever could defend them. The new world had given them a chance to build something better.

But somewhere inside the caravan's network, a dead intelligence had already bought a little piece of the future.

And it had paid with borrowed light.
