SURVIVOR FIELD MANUAL · RESEARCH DOSSIER 015 · 28 NOVEMBER 2026

WORLD LIBERTY FINANCIAL: THE 95% DRAWDOWN, TOKEN ECONOMICS AND THE PRICE OF TRUST

An evidence-led investigation of the Trump-family-associated crypto venture, its WLFI governance token, USD1 stablecoin, ownership disclosures, market shock and unresolved accountability questions.

EXECUTIVE FINDING

On 28 November 2026, market trackers recorded an exceptionally sharp WLFI sell-off. A CoinGlass snapshot at 07:35 UTC showed about $0.02014 per token, approximately 95.6% below the reported $0.46 all-time high. The drawdown is verifiable; the social-media claim that this alone proves fraud is not. The available record instead points to a combination of concentrated token allocations, complex unlock terms, collateral use, political exposure, litigation and a sudden liquidity shock.

Abstract

This dossier examines World Liberty Financial (WLF) and its WLFI token using a document-first method. It separates (1) statements made by the project, (2) disclosures in a public-company filing, (3) market-data snapshots, and (4) allegations made by litigants or political investigators. The purpose is not to pronounce a legal verdict, but to show what the public record establishes, what it suggests, and what remains unproven.

Research cut-off: 28 November 2026. Market values are time-sensitive snapshots, not a permanent quote. This is an independent research article, not a peer-reviewed academic paper, investment recommendation or legal opinion.

1. What is World Liberty Financial?

World Liberty Financial presents itself as a decentralised-finance ecosystem. Its product family includes the WLFI governance token and USD1, a dollar-referenced stablecoin. These are different instruments: WLFI is a governance and ecosystem token whose market price can fluctuate sharply; USD1 is designed to maintain a one-dollar value and depends on reserve, custody, issuance and redemption arrangements.

The project’s own governance disclosures describe a material economic connection to entities affiliated with Donald Trump and members of his family. The disclosure states that DT Marks DEFI LLC owns approximately 38% of the equity interests in WLF Holdco LLC, which holds the membership interest in the operating entity and the rights to net protocol revenues (excluding net proceeds from WLFI token sales). It also says DT Marks DEFI LLC and certain family members hold 22.5 billion WLFI tokens and that the affiliate is entitled to 75% of WLFI token-sale proceeds after specified deductions. These are the project’s own published statements; readers should consult the complete agreements and subsequent filings for context.

Source: World Liberty Financial — Governance and disclosures; official Gold Paper / offering disclosures.

2. Token supply: the allocation is the first risk map

Official WLFI documentation says the token launched with an initial fixed supply of 100 billion. The published allocation is 33.893 billion for token sales (33.893%), 32.6 billion for community growth and incentives (32.6%), 30 billion for co-founders (30%), and 3.507 billion for team and advisers (3.507%). Public sale rounds were described at $0.015 and $0.05 per token. The same documentation notes that some allocations were locked and that founder/team unlock arrangements were subject to later decisions.

Why does this matter? A token can have a fixed maximum supply and still carry substantial future supply risk. Locked tokens are not the same as burned tokens. A vesting schedule reduces immediate sell pressure but does not eliminate it; it moves potential supply into the future. Treasury holdings, strategic allocations, sale allocations and circulating supply must therefore be tracked separately.

The April 2026 governance proposal covered 17.04 billion locked early-supporter tokens and 45.24 billion founder, team, adviser and partner tokens — 62.28 billion in total. Under the proposal, early supporters could opt into a two-year cliff followed by a two-year linear vest. Founder/team/partner holders could opt into a two-year cliff and three-year vest, with a 10% burn of that cohort if they accepted the terms. The proposal’s own text says non-participating holders could remain locked indefinitely. This is a proposal and its terms should not be treated as proof that every eligible holder opted in or that every token was burned.

Source: WLFI governance proposal, 15 April 2026.

3. The 28 November market shock

CoinGlass reported a WLFI all-time high of $0.46 on 1 September 2025. Its 28 November 2026 snapshot at 07:35 UTC showed approximately $0.02014, a 24-hour decline of about 51.56% and a drawdown of roughly 95.6% from that high. The calculation is straightforward: 1 − (0.02014 ÷ 0.46) ≈ 0.9562. The Telegram screenshot supplied for this investigation shows a chart near $0.01926, consistent with the same extreme sell-off window.

A 95% drawdown is a severe market event, but it does not by itself establish why the price fell, who sold, whether any law was broken or whether a project is fraudulent. Price, trading volume, order-book depth, futures liquidations, token unlocks and wallet flows are different datasets. A rigorous attribution requires transaction-level evidence and a reproducible time series.

4. What the public-company filing reveals — and what it does not

AI Financial Corporation’s Form 10-Q for the period ended 26 September 2026 reports that the company held 7,283,585,650 WLFI tokens. It recorded their fair value at approximately $417.4 million, against a cost basis of about $1.457 billion, and recognised an unrealised loss of approximately $637.2 million for the 39-week period. The filing valued the tokens at $0.05731263 each at 26 September 2026.

The same filing reported a $503.4 million net loss from continuing operations and a $13.6 million working-capital deficit for the 39 weeks ended 26 September 2026. It also stated that the company had received a margin call after quarter-end and transferred 343.4 million WLFI tokens on 5 November 2026 to wallets jointly controlled with its counterparty. Crucially, the filing cautions that the quoted fair value of its WLFI holdings is not equivalent to unrestricted cash. The filing is evidence about AI Financial Corporation’s financial position; it is not a consolidated financial statement of World Liberty Financial LLC and should not be described as one.

5. WLFI is not USD1: do not mix the risks

USD1 is a separate stablecoin product. Its stated objective is to track one U.S. dollar; its safety depends on reserve assets, custody, redemption rights, operational controls and the quality of attestations. BitGo says it publishes monthly reserve attestations, with reports listed through September 2026 at the time of this research. WLF’s documentation also describes a proof-of-reserves dashboard, while warning that the live dashboard is informational and that formal monthly reports should be consulted.

Sources: BitGo USD1 attestations; WLF USD1 proof-of-reserves documentation.

A stablecoin reserve report does not guarantee the market price of WLFI. Conversely, a WLFI price crash does not automatically prove that USD1 reserves are deficient. Each claim requires evidence specific to the instrument.

6. Buybacks, governance and the limits of a support narrative

In November 2026, World Liberty Financial said it intended to continue WLFI buybacks, with purchases potentially funded from fees associated with USD1 reserves and other sources. CoinDesk reported that the project had disclosed $65.6 million in open-market buybacks over the preceding six months. Buybacks can reduce circulating supply or support demand, but they do not guarantee a floor price, and the economic effect depends on the size, timing, funding source, execution venue and disclosure quality.

Source: CoinDesk, 6 November 2026. Governance participation should also be examined: the project’s April proposal reported voting participation between 2.7 billion and 11.1 billion WLFI across six proposals, against a much larger locked-token base.

7. Legal and political scrutiny: allegations are not verdicts

Justin Sun and affiliated entities filed a civil action against World Liberty Financial in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California on 21 April 2026. The docket identifies the case as a contract dispute. Sun’s claims about frozen tokens and contractual rights are allegations in litigation; World Liberty has disputed his position and brought its own claims. The docket available to this research records procedural activity, not a final finding that either side’s allegations are true.

Separately, Senator Richard Blumenthal’s office published a 5 November 2026 letter renewing a request for records concerning potential conflicts of interest and financial dealings connected to Trump-associated crypto ventures. The letter describes an ongoing congressional inquiry and requests production of records. An inquiry or records request is not a judicial finding of misconduct.

Sources: Sun et al. v. World Liberty Financial — docket; Senator Blumenthal’s 5 November 2026 letter.

9. Lessons for crypto investors: the survivor checklist

  1. Separate the issuer, protocol, token and treasury companies. Similar branding does not mean identical legal entities or financial statements.
  2. Read allocation and unlock documents. Track who controls supply, what is locked, what can be transferred and when.
  3. Check collateral exposure. Tokens pledged for loans can create liquidation and reflexive selling risk when prices fall.
  4. Do not confuse market cap with exit liquidity. A quoted valuation is not cash that every holder can realise at that price.
  5. Verify claims on-chain. Wallet labels, screenshots and social posts need transaction hashes, timestamps and independent attribution.
  6. Distinguish allegations from findings. Court complaints, congressional letters and press reports have different evidentiary weight.
  7. Stress-test a 90%+ drawdown. If a position cannot survive it financially or psychologically, its size is too large.

Conclusion

The WLFI episode is a useful case study in the collision of token economics, political branding, corporate finance and DeFi market structure. The public record supports a severe price collapse, unusually large and complex token allocations, disclosed economic links to Trump-affiliated entities, and significant mark-to-market pressure at a major corporate holder. It also documents unresolved litigation and congressional scrutiny.

What the public record reviewed here does not establish is a final legal finding of fraud, a proven coordinated insider dump, or a definitive explanation for every trade during the 28 November sell-off. The strongest conclusion is narrower and more useful: investors were exposed to a token whose value depended not only on protocol adoption, but also on governance, unlock decisions, collateral practices, political and legal risk, and confidence in the entities controlling the ecosystem.
