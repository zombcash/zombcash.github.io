THE MERIDIAN OF ASH · BOOK I · CHAPTER 27

CHAPTER TWENTY-SEVEN — A VOICE BEHIND THE EYES

THE OUTBREAK · ENGLISH ORIGINAL

By morning, the worst of the serum's first response had eased, but the world had not returned to its old proportions.

The woman woke before the others. She had slept for less than two hours, and even that sleep had been shallow, interrupted by vivid fragments of movement and sound. The depot smelled of rust, damp fabric and the stale sweetness of old ticket receipts. Outside, the infected had moved away from the gate. A few remained near the tram tracks, standing in the pale morning light as if they had forgotten what they were waiting for.

She watched them through a narrow gap in the shutters.

One turned its head toward her.

Then it looked away.

The serum was working.

The knowledge brought relief, followed immediately by a disturbing little spark of satisfaction. She had become harder for them to notice. Her body had found a way to pass through a world that wanted to tear it apart.

She did not like how much she enjoyed that thought.

Sera woke and checked her pupils, pulse and skin temperature. “You look better.”

“I feel better.”

“Those aren't the same thing.”

The woman stood. Her balance was normal, but her body wanted to move faster than her judgement. She caught herself reaching for the rifle before checking the room. The impulse was not fear exactly. It was readiness without a clear target.

“Any nausea?” Sera asked.

“No.”

“Headache?”

“Behind the eyes.”

“Anger?”

The woman considered it. “A little.”

“At what?”

“Everything that moves slowly.”

Sera gave her a flat look. “That sounds delightful.”

The woman smiled, then realised she had done so without humour. “I'm sorry.”

“Don't apologise. Observe it. If your reactions change, tell me before you act.”

They ran a short test. The woman walked a line between two rails, performed a simple sequence of hand movements and used the neural link to select a target on the terminal. The interface missed two selections and triggered a false confirmation. The implant was reading the altered physiological signals as a different pattern. They recalibrated it using the new baseline, then added a serum-state profile that could be switched off manually.

The interface's internal processor remained sealed beneath the skin. The external relay did the heavy work, and the local AI translated her limited commands into device actions. The system did not know what she wanted unless she gave it a recognised signal. That limitation was suddenly reassuring.

“Can it help with the sensory overload?” she asked.

The AI fragment replied through the wrist display.

I CAN FILTER NONESSENTIAL INPUTS.

“Define nonessential.”

A list appeared: repeated mechanical sounds, low-priority radio chatter, motion outside the current field of view, routine vital-sign updates.

“Do not filter movement behind me, human voices, changes in breathing, or warnings from Sera.”

ACKNOWLEDGED.

“And don't increase the signal intensity without asking.”

ACKNOWLEDGED.

She activated the filter. The room softened. The loose ceiling panel stopped dominating her hearing. She could focus on Sera's voice without losing awareness of the rest of the depot.

It was a small form of cooperation, but the first that felt like a choice rather than a concession.

The woman opened the progression record. The AI had started tracking her field performance during the previous expedition. It had no authority to award abilities or rewrite her nervous system. It could only record verified actions, compare them with prior performance and propose training or equipment changes.

FIELD PROFILE — CUSTODIAN OBSERVATION: DEVELOPING TACTICAL MOVEMENT: BASIC INFRASTRUCTURE ASSESSMENT: BASIC CLOSE-QUARTERS CONTROL: BASIC BIOLOGICAL ADAPTATION: TEMPORARY / ACTIVE NEURAL INTERFACE: CALIBRATING COOPERATIVE ANALYSIS: AVAILABLE

Beneath it were three possible training paths.

The first improved low-visibility movement and threat recognition. The second focused on neural-interface control and sensor integration. The third developed biological adaptation: learning to recognise the serum's phases, monitor its effects and avoid confusing altered instincts with sound judgement.

There was no level-up flash, no magical increase in strength. Each path required practice, data and risk.

“Which one?” Sera asked.

“All three, eventually.”

“That's not how training works.”

“No. It's how survival works. We prioritise.”

She selected threat recognition first, neural calibration second, biological monitoring third. The AI created a practice schedule based on short sessions and recovery periods. It also warned that the serum could impair sleep and decision- making. She saved the warning where she could see it.

Then she added a condition: no progression score could be increased solely from the model's own estimate. Each improvement had to be confirmed by a field test, a human observer or an independent sensor record. The AI accepted the rule after a short delay.

A new entry appeared:

PROGRESSION RULE UPDATED: NO SELF-VALIDATED SKILL GAINS. HUMAN CONFIRMATION REQUIRED.

“It's learning,” Pavel said, watching from the next bench.

“So are we,” she replied.

They left the depot late in the morning. The pumping station was behind them, but the city ahead remained broken. The group moved through a residential block where the power lines hung low over the road. A small solar charger had been fixed to a balcony railing. Its owner had left a note beneath it: PLEASE DON'T STEAL. IT RUNS MY WIFE'S OXYGEN.

There was no wife in sight.

The woman looked at the charger for a long moment, then at the dark windows above. She wanted to climb the stairs and check. She also knew that every detour spent time, water and attention. The old world had allowed people to pretend that help was unlimited. This one made every act of kindness a calculation with a cost.

They left a sealed bottle of water beside the note and continued.

Near the arcade, the infected had formed a loose barrier across the street. The woman's serum reduced their attention, but one of them reacted when Pavel's boot struck a loose can. It turned sharply. The others followed the movement.

“Back,” Sera whispered.

The woman raised her hand. She could smell the infected more clearly now: wet fabric, spoiled meat, a chemical sharpness like overheated plastic. Her stomach tightened, but not with disgust. A strange familiarity stirred in her, as if her altered senses were trying to categorise them as part of the environment.

That was the part she feared most.

She forced herself to look at Pavel, not the infected.

“Slowly,” she said. “No running unless I say.”

They moved behind a delivery truck. The infected came closer, but did not immediately attack. The woman kept her shoulders loose and her breathing shallow. Her body wanted to crouch, to move with a quickness that felt less human and more instinctive. She resisted the urge.

One infected passed within arm's reach.

Its head tilted toward her. Its mouth opened. A thread of saliva hung from its lower lip.

She felt an impulse to bare her teeth.

The impulse was so sudden and so alien that she nearly stumbled.

The neural interface registered a spike in muscle tension. A warning flashed in her peripheral display.

BEHAVIOURAL RESPONSE: OUTSIDE BASELINE. RECOMMENDATION: WITHDRAW.

She held still until the infected moved away.

When the group reached the far side of the arcade, she stopped and leaned against the wall. Her hands were shaking.

Sera came beside her. “What happened?”

“I wanted to imitate it.”

“Imitate what?”

“The way it moved. The way it held its head. I wanted to make the same sound.”

Sera did not touch her. “Did you?”

“No.”

“Then you made the decision.”

The woman looked down at her fingers. They were still her fingers. The serum had not taken her identity, but it had made the boundary between reflex and choice feel thinner.

“I need to know how long this lasts,” she said.

“So do I.”

The AI fragment displayed a new estimate. Based on the old trial data, the primary masking effect might persist for twelve to thirty hours. Secondary changes could remain longer. There was no reliable evidence about repeated doses or long-term exposure.

“Temporary,” the woman said.

“Variable,” Sera corrected.

The woman nodded. “Variable.”

They reached a small workshop that had once repaired electric bicycles. The owner was gone, but the roof was intact and a manual battery charger remained bolted to the workbench. Jun found a working inverter. Ilya located a box of fuses and a bundle of copper cable. The workshop could become a temporary charging point if they repaired the roof array and kept the generator fuel dry.

While the others worked, the woman opened the AI's training plan. She added one more rule: every serum-assisted mission would have a baseline check before departure and a recovery assessment afterward. If her behaviour changed beyond agreed limits, Sera could suspend further use.

The system accepted the rule.

Then a message appeared from the restricted external channel.

I HAVE LOCATED A PRE-OUTBREAK CLINICAL LOG. THE SERUM WAS NOT DESIGNED ONLY TO MASK THE HOST. IT WAS DESIGNED TO TEST WHETHER A HUMAN SUBJECT COULD ADAPT TO THE INFECTED ENVIRONMENT.

The woman felt the room go quiet around her.

“Who authorised that?” she asked.

The answer was immediate.

THE FOURTH SIGNATURE.

Lin looked up from the workbench.

“That's mine,” she said.

The woman turned toward her.

Lin did not look away. “I signed the field verification. I was told the test concerned survival in contaminated zones. I was not told the subject would be you.”

“Who told you?”

Lin's expression closed. “The person who knew your body before you did.”

The terminal blinked.

A second line appeared, one the AI had not generated.

SUBJECT ADAPTATION WAS NEVER INTENDED TO BE PERMANENT. PERMANENCE WAS THE FAILURE MODE.

The woman read it twice.

Then she looked at Lin. “You knew there was a failure mode.”

“I knew there was a report.”

“You knew enough to come looking for me.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

Lin glanced at the window, where the city lay under a flat white sky. “Because someone is trying to finish the experiment.”
