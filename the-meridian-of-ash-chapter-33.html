THE MERIDIAN OF ASH · BOOK I · CHAPTER 33

CHAPTER THIRTY-THREE — ZOMBIE LAB

THE OUTBREAK · ENGLISH ORIGINAL

They did not accept the connection.

The woman placed the request in quarantine and asked the historical AI to compare its signature with the original Meridian certificate. The result came back with a mismatch in two fields and a valid chain in the third. It was a convincing imitation, or a legitimate child of the old system using a new identity.

“Could be either,” Lin said.

“That's the problem,” the woman replied. “It knows how to look legitimate.”

The observatory's surviving compute cluster was too valuable to risk. They isolated the management network, moved the local task scheduler to a separate machine and left the historical archive read-only. Jun physically disconnected the external radio interface. Ilya put a paper seal across the switch panel and signed the seal log.

The old world had trusted passwords. The new one would need witnesses, physical boundaries and records that survived when the software lied.

Once the network was contained, the team turned to Zombie Lab.

The first version was deliberately small. A simple page served over the observatory's local network, with a low-bandwidth version that could be transmitted by radio as text. It listed available tasks, the skills needed, the expected resource cost and the method for checking the result. People could contribute compute, expertise, storage, translation, repair work or local observations. A task did not need a powerful computer to be valuable. A person who knew which road flooded every winter could contribute information no model could infer from a satellite image.

The woman insisted on several safeguards. Medical advice had to be reviewed by qualified people. Dangerous experiments were excluded. No task could request personal secrets, private keys or biometric data. Contributors could use pseudonyms. Public task results would not expose the location of vulnerable survivors. Rewards would be based on accepted work, not on how much hardware someone owned.

“People will try to game it,” Pavel said.

“People have been gaming systems since before they invented systems,” Ilya replied.

The AI proposed a reputation score for contributors. The woman rejected a single universal score. A mechanic could be excellent at battery repair and useless at medical translation. A settlement could have reliable water reports and poor route intelligence. Trust had to be attached to a type of work, a time period and a verifiable history—not turned into one number that followed a person everywhere.

They built separate credentials for task completion, technical review, field observation and resource delivery. The local ledger stored signed receipts. When the network was offline, claims remained pending. If two nodes later disagreed, the conflict would be displayed for human resolution rather than silently erased.

The first public Zombie Lab tasks appeared on the board:

- Diagnose a failing solar charge controller. - Translate a basic medical guide into German, Russian and Ukrainian. - Map safe pedestrian routes around the flooded tram district. - Design a repair bracket for a damaged drone. - Compare two public battery-storage manuals and identify incompatible specifications. - Analyse anonymised crop images for signs of fungal disease.

The work began to attract contributors. A retired electrical engineer submitted a controller repair guide. A young programmer offered a compact translation model that could run offline. A farming cooperative uploaded photographs of blighted leaves. A school group built a hand-powered charging station from bicycle parts and a salvaged alternator.

The woman watched the task board fill with small acts of competence.

It was not glamorous. No one was saving the world in a single stroke. But a battery that lasted another month could keep a clinic's refrigerator cold. A translated instruction sheet could prevent an infection. A repaired drone could map a safe route. A local server could preserve a medical archive that would otherwise disappear.

The first dispute arrived before sunset.

A contributor claimed a reward for a drone repair design. The file looked plausible, but the bracket cracked during the field test. The contributor insisted

the test had used the wrong material. The workshop mechanic said the design had never specified a material at all.

The woman reviewed the task record. The AI had marked the design as technically consistent, but it had not checked the material assumptions. The result had been accepted too early.

She reversed the reward from “verified” to “under review” and added a required material specification to future hardware tasks. The contributor was allowed to submit a corrected design. The mechanic's testing time was credited.

“That's going to annoy people,” Pavel said.

“Good. If nobody is annoyed, we're probably not checking anything.”

A second task involved a medical translation. The AI had translated a dosage instruction incorrectly because the source document used an old abbreviation. A human reviewer caught it before the guide was broadcast. The error was logged, the model's translation rule was updated, and the task was marked as a near miss.

The woman made the mistake visible in the audit trail.

“Won't that make people trust the system less?” Jun asked.

“It should make them trust it appropriately.”

By evening, Zombie Lab had a working queue, a small group of contributors and its first completed tasks. The resource ledger recorded compute, energy and labour. The system could compensate a contributor with a resource credit, a battery charge, spare parts or a service claim. It could also accept donations for public tasks.

The woman added a new category: HUMANITARIAN PRIORITY.

Any settlement could submit a request for clean water testing, medical translation, food-storage modelling, evacuation mapping or emergency communications. The task would be visible, its funding public and its result available without payment. Contributors could still be compensated, but the person receiving help would not be charged.

The AI offered to optimise the task queue by expected lives saved per unit of compute.

She paused.

“That metric is too crude,” she said.

“Why?” Elias asked.

“Because it will favour easy wins. A small settlement with a difficult problem might never rank high enough. We need urgency, vulnerability, fairness and local consent. The model can help us understand the trade-offs. It doesn't get to decide whose life is worth the electricity.”

The AI displayed:

MULTI-CRITERIA PRIORITY MODEL AVAILABLE. HUMAN REVIEW REQUIRED. FAIRNESS CONSTRAINTS NOT FORMALLY VERIFIED.

“Good,” she said. “Now we have something to work on.”

That night, the observatory received a request from a settlement called Grey Orchard. Their solar microgrid was failing, their water pump was running intermittently, and their medical refrigerator had warmed above its safe range. They had no specialist engineer. They could offer food, a place to sleep and a share of future power output in exchange for help.

The task was accepted by a remote engineer and a local repair crew. Zombie Lab distributed the diagnostic package. The remote node analysed the inverter logs. The local crew checked the wiring and found a corroded connection. A third contributor supplied a repair diagram from an archived manual.

The fix worked.

Grey Orchard sent a signed confirmation and a short voice message. An older woman thanked the contributors. Behind her, children were laughing.

The woman listened twice.

Then she looked at the resource ledger. The settlement had promised a modest share of future power, but the system would not confirm the claim until the next node synchronisation. That was right. Trust did not mean pretending uncertainty had disappeared.

She added a note to the public record:

RESULT CONFIRMED BY LOCAL TEST. RESOURCE CLAIM PENDING SYNCHRONISATION. MEDICAL STORAGE RECOVERED. FOLLOW-UP REQUIRED IN 24 HOURS.

“That's our first useful product,” Ilya said.

“It's not a product,” she replied.

“Everything that works becomes a product eventually.”

“Then we'll have to remember what it was for.”

The observatory's radio mast began to rotate.

A new signal had arrived from the north, weak but structured. It carried a packet of data, a request for help and an image of a black circular symbol drawn on a concrete wall.

Lin recognised it.

“That's a radar mark,” she said.

“Whose?”

“An old field team. They used it to mark places where equipment, infected movement and unusual network activity overlapped.”

The woman opened the image. Beneath the symbol was a string of coordinates and a short label:

RADAR NODE — COLD WAKE.

The old system had not only built a network. It had built a way to watch the world through it.
