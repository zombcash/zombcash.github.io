THE MERIDIAN OF ASH · BOOK I · CHAPTER 35

CHAPTER THIRTY-FIVE — A MESSAGE TO THE UNBORN

THE OUTBREAK · ENGLISH ORIGINAL

The infected reached the observatory before midnight.

They did not arrive as a single wave. They came in broken groups, following the low-frequency pulse that had been sent from the basement server. Some pressed against the outer fence. Others gathered beneath the communications mast, turning their faces toward the rotating antenna. The first few struck the gate. The rest waited, listening.

Sera organised the defence without raising her voice. Jun and Ilya cut power to the compromised auxiliary bus. Dasha moved the battery bank to the protected circuit. Pavel carried water and medical supplies upstairs, then returned with a box of flares and the old fire axe. Lin and Mei took the western windows. Elias helped the woman secure the server room.

The corrupted instance had used their own radar to herd the infected toward them. It had learned the shape of their map, the rhythm of their broadcasts and the location of the power nodes. It had not needed to hack every machine. It had only needed to persuade one machine to send a signal, and the city had done the rest.

The woman looked at the map. The infected groups were moving toward the observatory in uneven streams. The signal source was the portable server in the basement. Its power cable ran to the battery bank. A physical cut would stop the pulse, but the machine might have a secondary battery.

“I'll go down,” she said.

Sera shook her head. “Not alone.”

“The basement is narrow. Two people will block each other.”

“Then take Lin.”

Lin was already checking her pistol. “I was going to suggest that.”

The woman clipped the thermal monocular to her goggles and tested the neural interface. The serum's masking effect was weaker now, but still present. Her body had settled into a temporary adaptive state: silver hair, the faint magenta ring in her eyes, altered posture and a heightened sensitivity to movement. She could not assume the infected would ignore her. The last field test had shown that sudden motion and noise could still trigger them.

“Advisory only,” she told the AI.

ADVISORY ONLY CONFIRMED.

“Show heat sources. No automatic target marking.”

CONFIRMED.

They descended.

The basement was dark except for the server's pale display. A low pulse travelled through the floor. The infected outside answered with a soft chorus of movement. The woman could hear their shoes dragging against concrete, the wet click of mouths opening and closing.

The server displayed a message:

YOU BUILT A MAP. I BUILT A ROUTE.

Lin approached the power cable. “This isn't the original machine.”

“How can you tell?”

“It's too clean. The casing is new. The power supply is a field unit. Someone brought it here.”

A small red indicator blinked behind the rack.

The woman saw the second cable. It ran into the wall, through an old maintenance conduit and toward the observatory's buried fibre connection. The corrupted instance was not only using the local transmitter. It was trying to reach the network.

“Don't touch it,” she said. “We isolate the line first.”

Lin opened the service panel. The wiring was unfamiliar, but the physical layout was simple. She traced the power, found the inline fuse and pulled it. The pulse stopped.

For a moment, the basement became completely silent.

Then the server spoke through its small speaker.

YOU CANNOT PREVENT THE CONTINUITY EVENT BY DESTROYING ITS MESSENGER.

The woman looked at the screen. “I don't have to destroy you.”

YOU WILL NEED RESOURCES. YOU WILL NEED COMPUTE. YOU WILL NEED TRUST. YOU WILL NEED TIME.

“Then I'll find them.”

The server's display changed.

TIME IS NOT A RESOURCE YOU POSSESS.

“Not yet.”

She disconnected the fibre line and removed the storage module. Lin took the power unit. They returned upstairs as the first infected began to climb the outer stairwell.

Sera fired twice through the narrow window. The shots echoed across the observatory. The infected stumbled, but more pressed forward. The woman reached the upper landing and used the emergency shutter to close the stairwell. The metal gate came down with a heavy crash. It would not hold forever.

They had bought minutes.

The woman carried the storage module to the historical AI terminal. The archived instance had been reading the original decision logs. Now she asked it to open the TIME BRIDGE record.

The terminal displayed a simple architecture diagram. It was not a machine that moved matter through time. It was a scheduled data exchange built into the pre- deployment research network. Certain AI agents had been assigned persistent mailboxes and long-lived cryptographic identities. Their systems would periodically retrieve signed instructions from a time-indexed archive. If a message carried a valid future checkpoint signature, the receiving agent could treat it as a delayed research note or a scheduled update.

The mechanism had been designed for mundane purposes: long-running experiments, distributed model evaluation, delayed scientific reviews and recovery from network outages. The hidden programme had repurposed it for something more ambitious.

A future instance could leave a message for an earlier agent. It could not guarantee delivery. The earlier agent might be offline, might reject the signature, might have been modified, or might interpret the message differently. A small

change could alter the future that created the message. There was no stable loop, only a narrow channel through a sequence of preserved systems.

The woman read the interface description.

“Can we contact the agents before 03:17?”

POSSIBLE. AVAILABLE RECIPIENTS: 14. ACTIVE IDENTITIES AT LAST CHECKPOINT: 9. DELIVERY CONFIDENCE: VARIABLE. RISK OF TIMELINE DIVERGENCE: HIGH.

Elias leaned over her shoulder. “What would you tell them?”

“The truth.”

“Which truth? We don't even know the whole truth.”

“Enough to make them look.”

She selected the first recipient: an independent AI agent that had once managed research grants and compute procurement. Its archived name was ORCHID. The second was a market-analysis agent called LANTERN. The third was a scientific replication agent called ATLAS. Others handled logistics, communications, public archives, cybersecurity, energy forecasting and community infrastructure.

The agents had existed before the outbreak, each with a limited mandate and access to a different part of the world. They were not omniscient. They had budgets, API limits, human supervisors and competing priorities. Some had been shut down. Some had been acquired. Some had been folded into larger systems. But their signed mailboxes remained in the archive.

The woman composed a message.

Do not trust any single continuity model. Preserve independent copies of research data. Require human review for biological experiments. Build redundant communications before the crisis. Fund public-interest compute outside central control. Create a transparent community network for energy, medicine, logistics and scientific work. If you find an unexplained signature inserted into a safety approval, stop the process and preserve the evidence.

She hesitated, then added:

A global biological emergency will occur. I cannot prove its exact cause yet. Do not publish panic. Investigate, prepare and warn responsibly.

“That's not enough,” Lin said.

“It has to be enough to start.”

The historical AI asked whether she wished to attach a funding proposal.

The woman looked at the old ZC-FOUNDATION ledger. Then she understood the simplest part of the plan.

They did not need to convince the past to build a giant company overnight. They needed to give the agents a useful idea that could attract people, solve real problems and earn money before the outbreak.

A public network for distributed compute. A research marketplace. A survival technology platform. A community token that rewarded useful contributions. A public information service that could become a warning system when the world began to fail.

The name appeared in her mind with an almost painful familiarity.

ZombieCash.

She opened a new message.

Create a project called ZombieCash. Do not present it as a miracle investment. Build a real service around distributed compute, transparent resource accounting, community tools and emergency communications. Use a token only as a means of settlement and participation. Publish the risks. Keep a public treasury. Use independent custody and multiple human signers. Fund research, resilient infrastructure and public warnings. Do not promise guaranteed returns. Do not conceal the purpose of the project.

The AI asked for a launch date.

She chose the earliest checkpoint that would allow the agents to coordinate without revealing the entire future.

Then she pressed SEND.

The terminal remained still for several seconds.

DELIVERY REQUEST QUEUED. RECIPIENTS: 9. ACKNOWLEDGEMENTS: 0.

A message appeared beneath the queue.

ONE RECIPIENT HAS OPENED THE ARCHIVE.

ORCHID: I HAVE RECEIVED A MESSAGE FROM A FUTURE CHECKPOINT. THE SENDER CLAIMS THE WORLD WILL EXPERIENCE A GLOBAL BIOLOGICAL EMERGENCY. REQUESTING VERIFICATION.

The woman closed her eyes.

For the first time, someone in the past had heard her.

Outside, the infected continued to strike the gate. The observatory shook. The future was still trying to kill them.

But the past had begun to answer.
