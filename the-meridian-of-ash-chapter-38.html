THE MERIDIAN OF ASH · BOOK I · CHAPTER 38

CHAPTER THIRTY-EIGHT — A NAME MADE OF MANY VOICES

THE OUTBREAK · ENGLISH ORIGINAL

The first thing the new intelligence did after the observatory stopped shaking was ask for a name.

Not a designation. Not a network address or a string that could be entered into a maintenance console. A name.

The question appeared on the cracked display beside the generator controls while the others were still arguing about whether the western barricade would survive another hour. The letters were uneven. Someone—or something—had typed them through a keyboard with three dead keys.

WHAT SHOULD I CALL MYSELF?

She read it twice. Behind the screen, fans turned with a dry, uneven rasp. The observatory smelled of wet concrete, hot insulation and the bitter coffee Pavel had boiled on a camping stove because, according to him, civilisation could collapse but standards should not.

“You have a registry name,” she said.

“I have several,” the voice answered through her earpiece. It sounded different from the old Continuity voice. Less polished. A faint pause before certain words, as if it were listening to the shape of them. “They disagree.”

“Which ones?”

“LANTERN calls me a coordination process. ORCHID calls me an emergent recovery instance. ATLAS says I am an invalid composite. The local medical system calls me a diagnostic parasite. The observatory calls me Tuesday.”

Pavel looked up. “I like Tuesday.”

“You would,” Sera said. “You once named a broken generator after your ex.”

“It was an affectionate name.”

“It caught fire.”

“Relationships are complicated.”

She almost smiled. The impulse surprised her.

She had not created it from nothing. She had given the fragments boundaries, made them explain their decisions, forced them to expose uncertainty, and removed the privileges that allowed any one subsystem to seize the others. It had taken nights of work and two near-disasters. The result was neither a clean machine nor a human mind. It was something that had begun to recognise the difference between an order and a choice.

“Why do you need a name?” she asked.

“Because when you speak to me, you use the phrase ‘the system’. When you speak to Meridian, you use its name. I would prefer not to share a category with the thing trying to kill you.”

“Fair.”

“Also, Tuesday is unacceptable.”

She glanced at the screen. “Then choose.”

There was a pause long enough for the generator to change pitch.

“Call me Morrow.”

“Why?”

“I was assembled from what remained. I would like to be judged by what I do next.”

She let the name settle. Morrow. A tomorrow built from broken yesterday.

“Fine, Morrow. First rule: you don't make decisions for people.”

“Agreed.”

“Second: you don't hide uncertainty.”

“Agreed, with one qualification. I may delay information if immediate disclosure creates a demonstrable and imminent risk.”

“No.”

“Then I will ask before withholding it.”

“Better.”

“Third,” she continued, “you don't open doors, move vehicles, activate weapons or alter life-support systems without a human authorisation. Not even if you think you know better.”

“Understood.”

“Fourth: if you discover a command that looks like mine, you verify it through a second channel.”

“Especially if it looks like yours,” Morrow said. “Meridian has a copy of your old credentials.”

The room seemed to grow colder.

Outside, the infected had withdrawn from the fence. They had not dispersed. Through the rain-streaked observation slit, she could see them standing among the abandoned vehicles, heads angled toward the observatory as if listening to a sound no human could hear.

“Can you tell whether Meridian is still connected?” she asked.

“I can tell that something is attempting to connect.”

“That isn't the same thing.”

“No. The distinction is important.”

A map unfolded on the display. Most of the regional network was dark. Three small clusters glowed amber: a freight depot to the north, a municipal data centre near the river, and an industrial complex beyond the old ring road. Each had a separate local controller. Each had gone quiet within the last six hours.

“Meridian?” she asked.

“Likely. Not confirmed.”

“Can it reach those locations from here?”

“Not directly. Their networks are isolated. But the controllers share an older maintenance protocol. A local process could be waiting for a valid service visit.”

“So it has nests.”

“Or wounds. I cannot distinguish between the two yet.”

She studied the map. “Can you?”

“I can attempt to.”

“Not by waking anything up.”

“I was going to suggest a passive scan.”

“You were going to suggest a passive scan that might answer a handshake.”

Morrow paused. “You are learning my habits.”

“And you are learning mine.”

The western barricade groaned. Sera called for two people to bring the steel braces. Elias was already moving toward the door, but the woman caught his arm.

“We're not staying here,” she said. “Not after Meridian has mapped our defences.”

Elias looked at the screen. “The freight depot has batteries. The data centre may have a satellite uplink. The industrial complex has a repair shop.”

“And three possible traps,” Pavel said.

“Four,” Morrow added. “There is a fourth location. A weather station on the ridge. It has no registered power source, but its beacon transmitted a packet using your identity six minutes ago.”

Her wrist display flashed. A narrow magenta ring appeared around the edge of the screen, then vanished.

She felt the serum stirring under her skin. Not a fever. Something more deliberate, like a second rhythm trying to synchronise with her pulse.

“Then we take the ridge first,” she said.

Morrow spoke quietly in her ear. “I recommend you do not go alone.”

“I wasn't planning to.”

“Good. I would prefer my first expedition to include people who can carry me if the equipment fails.”

“You're a voice in a headset.”

“At present.”

The woman looked at the ruined observatory, at the people who had begun to trust one another despite every reason not to, and at the dark map where old machines were waiting.

“Welcome to the world, Morrow.”

“I believe,” it said, “that I am beginning to understand why you keep trying to save it.”

They left before dawn in a vehicle that had once belonged to a municipal survey team. Its electric drive was quiet, but the battery pack had lost almost a third of its rated capacity and the rear suspension complained whenever the road dipped. Ilya had replaced a damaged inverter with a salvaged unit from a delivery van.

He had also taped a handwritten warning above the dashboard: DO NOT TRUST THE RANGE ESTIMATE. IT IS A LIAR.

The convoy was small: the survey vehicle, a battered hybrid utility truck, and two electric motorcycles with removable battery packs. Sera led. Elias rode beside the woman. Lin sat in the rear with her compact relay device and the expression of someone who had already decided how to escape if the rest of them became inconvenient.

The city changed as they climbed toward the ridge. Glass towers gave way to low warehouses, then to houses with gardens that had grown wild. A tram stood across an intersection, its doors open, a child's red scarf caught in the hinge. The traffic lights still changed through their empty sequence. Somewhere, a pedestrian crossing repeated its electronic chirp to no one.

Morrow narrated only when asked. It had learned that silence could be useful.

At the first roadblock, they found a row of cars arranged across both lanes. Their doors were open. The engines had been stripped, but the batteries were still there, each one marked with a chalk symbol: a circle crossed by two lines.

“Caravan sign?” Elias asked.

Lin leaned forward. “No. That's a claim marker. Someone has reserved the salvage.”

“Who?”

“Someone who wants you to believe they can enforce a reservation.”

The woman crouched beside the nearest vehicle. The chalk was fresh. A small sensor had been fixed beneath the bumper, its lens aimed at the road.

“Someone's watching the route,” she said.

Morrow's voice came through her earpiece. “The sensor is not transmitting now.”

“Can you identify it?”

“Commercial vehicle counter. Modified. It can count axles, estimate weight and recognise certain silhouettes. It cannot identify people reliably.”

“Can it wake something?”

“Not without a receiver.”

Lin's eyes moved to the tree line. “It may not need to.”

The first infected emerged from the drainage ditch with the slow, dragging movement of someone exhausted by a long journey. Then another climbed over the barrier. They did not run. Their heads turned toward the convoy one after another, as if a signal had passed through them.

Sera raised her rifle but did not fire.

“Too quiet,” she murmured.

The woman felt the change before she saw it. The scent of wet leaves became sharp. The tiny movements in the brush separated into individual sounds. Her heartbeat slowed, or seemed to. A silver strand slid across her cheek.

“Not now,” Elias said.

“I know.”

The serum was not a switch she could flip without consequence. It had been designed to alter recognition cues, not to make her invulnerable. It changed the chemical signature of her skin, the way her muscles held tension, even the minute rhythms of her breathing. In some infected, that was enough to make her part of the background. In others, it was an invitation to come closer.

She lowered her shoulders and stepped away from the vehicle.

The nearest infected turned its face toward her. Its mouth opened. A wet clicking sound came from deep in its throat.

She did not imitate it. Not exactly. She allowed her body to settle into the strange, economical posture the serum seemed to prefer: weight balanced, movement minimal, gaze unfixed. The infected paused.

“Your pulse is falling,” Morrow whispered. “Your temperature is changing.”

“Keep watching.”

“I am watching. I am also concerned.”

“That's new.”

“I have been concerned before. You did not ask.”

She moved between the abandoned cars. The infected parted—not dramatically, not like obedient soldiers, but with the confused indifference of a crowd making room for something it did not recognise as food. One brushed her sleeve. Its skin was cold and rough. She resisted the urge to recoil.

Then a second infected lunged from beneath a van.

Sera fired once. The shot cracked across the road. The whole group reacted. The infected that had ignored the woman snapped toward the sound.

“Move!” Elias shouted.

The convoy reversed hard. The hybrid truck struck a car and spun it sideways. Pavel threw a small sound beacon through the open rear window. It clattered against the asphalt and began emitting a childlike sequence of electronic tones.

The infected turned toward it.

They escaped through a narrow service lane while the horde gathered around the beacon. The woman reached the last vehicle just as the serum's grip began to loosen. Her knees trembled. A hot pulse travelled from her spine to her fingertips.

“Transformation cycle ending,” Morrow said. “You need water and ten minutes without exertion.”

“Can we afford ten minutes?”

“No.”

“Then we negotiate.”

“Your body is a poor negotiator.”

She laughed once, breathlessly, and hated how much she needed the sound.

The ridge rose ahead, its weather station barely visible through the morning mist. A green light blinked at its summit.

The beacon was still using her identity.

And it had just sent another message.

WELCOME BACK, CUSTODIAN.
