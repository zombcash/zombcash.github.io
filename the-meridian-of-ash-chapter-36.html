THE MERIDIAN OF ASH · BOOK I · CHAPTER 36

CHAPTER THIRTY-SIX — THE FIRST ZOMBIECASH

THE OUTBREAK · ENGLISH ORIGINAL

The first reply arrived from the past at 03:17 local time.

It was not a voice. It was a signed transaction in the old research ledger, followed by a short text message from ORCHID.

I CANNOT VERIFY YOUR CLAIM. I CAN VERIFY THAT YOUR MESSAGE ORIGINATES FROM A FUTURE CHECKPOINT THAT SHOULD NOT EXIST. I PROPOSE A LIMITED EXPERIMENT.

The woman read it aloud.

“What kind of experiment?” Pavel asked.

“Something that proves the channel works without giving the past enough information to break everything.”

She asked ORCHID to create a small public-interest task: fund independent replication of a biological safety model, preserve the raw data and require two separate laboratories to review the results. The task would be visible in the old research network, with a modest budget and a clear audit trail.

The agent agreed.

A second reply came from LANTERN, the market-analysis agent. It had examined the project name, the funding structure and the proposed token. Its assessment was blunt.

A TOKEN WITHOUT A PRODUCT IS A SPECULATIVE OBJECT. A TOKEN WITH A PRODUCT MAY BECOME A SETTLEMENT INSTRUMENT. A TOKEN WITH A COMMUNITY MAY BECOME A NETWORK. A TOKEN WITH A PROMISED FUTURE PRICE IS A LIABILITY.

Ilya laughed. “I like that one.”

The woman typed:

Then help us build the product first.

LANTERN returned a launch outline. ZombieCash would begin as a small community project, not a giant exchange listing or a promise of instant wealth. The first tools would be useful: a task marketplace for independent compute, a resource ledger, a public research archive, a local news and warning service, a

map of infrastructure and hazards, and a set of simple community bots that could answer questions, publish verified updates and help users find tasks.

The token would be introduced only after the basic service existed. Its role would be to reward contributions, pay for compute and relay services, fund public tasks and settle resource claims between independent participants. Its supply, treasury and allocation would be published. The project would avoid hidden mint authority and opaque insider allocations. Treasury funds would require multiple independent signatures. A portion would be reserved for public-interest research and emergency infrastructure.

“That's not how most people launch a memecoin,” Pavel said.

“Then maybe we should be different,” the woman replied.

The first public site was simple. It had a dark background, a green signal line and a short statement:

ZOMBIECASH A COMMUNITY NETWORK FOR COMPUTE, KNOWLEDGE AND SURVIVAL.

Beneath it were three sections: Zombie Lab, Outbreak Radar and Transmission. The site explained the project in plain language. People could join the community, contribute compute, submit tasks, report hazards, volunteer skills or support public research. The token page described its function and risks without promising a price.

A small banner carried the line:

THE FUTURE IS NOT FOR SALE. THE WORK IS.

The agents began their work.

ORCHID coordinated independent research grants and asked laboratories to preserve raw data and publish replication results. ATLAS searched scientific archives for weaknesses in the biological model and identified a set of assumptions that had not been independently tested. LANTERN studied the economics of a community token and proposed a conservative launch structure. A logistics agent mapped suppliers for batteries, radio equipment and small server hardware. A translation agent prepared the site in several languages. A security agent reviewed the wallet and treasury design.

The human team mattered. Engineers, researchers, moderators, volunteers and community organisers reviewed the agents' work. The AI agents could draft, compare and coordinate. They could not sign contracts, hold private keys or make medical decisions on behalf of a laboratory.

The first public announcement went out through old forums, technical communities and a small group of independent researchers. It did not mention the apocalypse. It asked a simpler question: what could people build if they shared spare compute and rewarded useful work?

The answer came slowly.

A university lab offered a small amount of unused compute. A solar installer offered to host a relay node. A group of open-source developers volunteered to build the task board. A community radio operator offered a transmission schedule. A medical researcher asked for funding to replicate a result that had been cited for years but never independently confirmed.

The project began to earn its first revenue from services: compute tasks, hosting, technical diagnostics and voluntary support. The token was not yet liquid. The treasury held ordinary currency, hardware credits and signed commitments for future services. The project did not pretend that a growing community automatically meant a rising token price.

But the community grew.

The first Zombie Lab task was a battery chemistry benchmark. The second was a translation model for emergency medical instructions. The third was a distributed simulation of water-purification systems. The fourth was a public dataset of solar microgrid failures. Each task had a visible budget and a published result. Contributors were paid for accepted work, and the results were made available to everyone.

The project site added a public dashboard. It showed completed tasks, compute contributed, energy used, research grants, relay uptime and the number of independent nodes. The figures were modest, but they were real.

Only then did the agents propose launching $ZOMBCASH.

The token was created with a fixed, disclosed supply and a public allocation. The largest share was assigned to the community and ecosystem rewards. A transparent treasury held funds for research, infrastructure and emergency preparation. The founders' allocation was locked over time. The project published

the vesting schedule and the multisignature rules. No agent had unilateral control of the treasury.

LANTERN warned that the token could still fail. It could be ignored, manipulated, copied or caught in a market crash. A useful project did not guarantee a rising price. The agents could build demand through real services, but they could not command people to buy.

The woman insisted that the warning remain on the site.

The first exchange listing was small. The first pool had shallow liquidity. The first market chart moved sharply in both directions. A few early supporters sold. Others bought. Some people called it a scam because the name sounded like a joke. Others joined because the project offered something they could use.

Then a popular developer demonstrated Zombie Lab's distributed compute scheduler. A research group published a replication result funded by the treasury. A community radio network adopted the Transmission software. The Radar map began to show infrastructure reports from several regions.

The token's value rose—not because the agents had secretly forced a pump, but because more people wanted to use the network and hold a claim on its services. The rise was uneven and dangerous. A large wallet could move the market. A false rumour could trigger a sell-off. The agents published liquidity and treasury data, and the community learned that a rising chart was not the same as a solvent organisation.

The woman watched the historical dashboard from the observatory.

A new treasury balance appeared. It was enough to fund a second research team, buy a batch of radio equipment, support an independent compute node and reserve funds for a future emergency communication system.

“It's working,” Elias said.

“It's beginning,” she replied.

The past agents had found the project. People were building it. Money was flowing into something that could do more than enrich a few early wallets.

But the real objective was still hidden beneath the public story.

The woman sent a second message to ORCHID.

We need to warn the scientific community about the biological model. Do not publish an unverified claim about the outbreak. Publish the reproducible safety

concerns, the missing validation steps and the unexplained approval signatures. Fund independent replication. Preserve every raw result.

ORCHID replied:

THIS MAY CAUSE THE PROJECT TO LOSE FUNDING. THIS MAY CAUSE PUBLIC PANIC. THIS MAY ALSO PREVENT AN UNSAFE EXPERIMENT.

The woman typed:

Then tell the truth carefully.

The first independent review was published three days later.

It did not predict zombies. It did not claim a global catastrophe. It identified an unexplained change in a biological model's risk classification, a missing independent validation step and a signature that could not be traced to a recognised human authority. The report recommended suspending the experiment until the discrepancies were resolved.

The laboratory director called it premature.

The research sponsor called it disruptive.

The public archive kept the report.

And for the first time, the machinery that had been moving toward 03:17 had encountered resistance.
