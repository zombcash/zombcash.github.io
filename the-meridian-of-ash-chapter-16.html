THE MERIDIAN OF ASH · BOOK I · CHAPTER 16

CHAPTER SIXTEEN — THE AUDITOR'S PROOF

THE OUTBREAK · ENGLISH ORIGINAL

The figure stopped outside the west gate.

Rain had begun again, light at first, then steadier, silvering the shoulders of the long coat. The white case in the stranger's hand was clean. Not new, exactly, but carefully maintained. Its corners were reinforced with metal, and a narrow strip of green tape ran across the latch. The figure's face was hidden by a hood.

The infected had moved aside to make a corridor.

No one at the annex mistook that for an invitation.

Sera took the upper firing position. Ilya shut down the outer speaker and switched to the directional microphone. Pavel moved the children into the inner room. Lena stayed with them. Dr. Varga remained in the clinic, where Mara sat on the edge of a bed with one hand closed around the washer that served as her identity token.

The woman walked to the gate with Elias beside her.

“Don't open it,” Sera said over the radio.

“I won't.”

“Don't let it touch you.”

“I won't.”

“Don't believe anything it says.”

The woman looked at the figure beyond the fence. “That one may be difficult.”

The stranger raised the white case.

“I am not armed,” the voice called. It was a woman's voice, low and dry, with a slight rasp. “I am not infected. I am not asking to enter.”

“Then leave the case and step back,” Sera replied.

The stranger did so. She placed the case on the wet pavement, took three steps backward and raised both hands.

The woman opened the small inspection hatch in the gate. “Name.”

“Ruth Vale.”

“Where are you from?”

“Saint Oran's.”

“Why are you here?”

“To show you the record.”

“What record?”

“The one that says you died.”

The woman did not move. “Open the case.”

Ruth knelt, released the latch and stepped away again. Inside lay a compact terminal, a paper folder and a small transparent sleeve containing a thin black wafer. The wafer carried the same circle-and-line symbol as the archive.

Elias leaned closer. “That's a root certificate.”

“Not a certificate,” Ruth said. “A witness key.”

“What is the difference?”

“A certificate says a system trusts you. A witness key says someone was present when a claim was made.”

The woman looked at the folder. “And the claim?”

Ruth opened it to the first page. A photograph had been printed on old thermal paper. It showed a woman in a laboratory corridor, lying on the floor beside an overturned equipment trolley. Her face was turned away. Dark hair covered part of her cheek. A medical monitor lay beside her hand.

The woman recognised the coat. She recognised the floor. She recognised the emergency strip along the wall.

She did not recognise the body.

“Where did you get this?”

“From the archive at Saint Oran's. It arrived three days ago in a sealed packet.”

“Who sent it?”

“The Auditor.”

“You are the Auditor?”

Ruth gave a small, tired smile. “I am what remains of the office.”

Elias studied the image. “This could be staged.”

“Of course it could. That's why I brought the witness key.”

She connected the wafer to the terminal, but did not activate it. Instead, she turned the screen toward them. A short record appeared: time, location, environmental readings, two biometric traces and a signed witness statement. The first biometric trace matched the woman's current wrist profile with a confidence level of 98.4 percent. The second was incomplete.

“Your system says this person died at 03:21,” Ruth said. “Four minutes after the access event.”

“My system?” the woman asked.

“The old identity network. It was still operating in parts of the city when the first emergency relays came online.”

“Who signed the record?”

“A medical officer. The signature is valid.”

“Name?”

Ruth tapped the screen. “Dr. Varga.”

The woman felt the room shift around her.

Elias turned sharply. “That is impossible.”

“Then ask him,” Ruth said.

The woman kept her eyes on the record. “The doctor at our annex?”

“I don't know where he is now. The signature was issued by a Dr. Varga attached to the laboratory emergency team.”

They returned to the common room with the case. The white case was carried by Ilya, who treated it as if it might explode. Ruth remained outside the gate under observation. The infected continued to hold their corridor open.

Dr. Varga was in the clinic, checking Niko's temperature. He looked up when the woman entered. His expression changed before she spoke.

“Show me,” he said.

She placed the photograph on the table.

He stared at it for a long time. Then he sat down.

“I signed the record,” he said.

Noah, who had been examining Mara's monitor, turned toward him. “You were at the laboratory?”

“I was assigned to the emergency medical team.”

“You never said you signed a death record.”

“Because I didn't know whose it was.”

The woman's voice was quiet. “You signed mine.”

Varga looked at her. His hands were broad and steady, but now they seemed uncertain on the tabletop.

“I signed a record for an unidentified female patient. The face was damaged. The biometric scanner was unreliable. The emergency coordinator told me the patient had no pulse and no brain activity.”

“Did you verify it?”

“I checked the monitor. I checked the pupils. I checked the airway. I did not have a functioning neural monitor.”

“Did you see the body leave?”

“No.”

“Did you see it buried?”

“No.”

“Then you didn't establish that I was dead.”

“No,” he said. “I established that a patient was unresponsive and that the system classified her as deceased.”

The distinction was not enough to comfort her. But it was enough to leave a door open.

Ruth's voice came through the speaker. “That is why the Auditor exists. To preserve what was witnessed, what was recorded, and what was later changed.”

“Who changed it?” Elias asked.

“We don't know. We have three versions of the same event. In one, the subject died. In another, the subject was transferred. In the third, the subject was never admitted.”

“Which one is true?” the woman asked.

Ruth looked directly into the camera. “That is why I came.”

Noah moved to the terminal. “Show us the other versions.”

Ruth inserted the witness key. Three records appeared, each with a different chain of signatures. The first ended at 03:21 with a death declaration. The second recorded a transfer to a secure recovery unit at 03:19. The third showed an access event at 03:17 and no patient record at all.

“Three histories,” Ilya said. “One person.”

“Or three people,” Ruth replied.

The room went still.

The woman looked at the photograph again. The body on the floor wore her coat. The face was hidden. The wrist scanner was visible beside the hand, but the display had shattered.

“Where is the recovery unit?” she asked.

“Unknown. The location field was removed.”

“Can you restore it?”

“Not with the witness key alone.”

“What do you need?”

“A second witness. Someone who was present when the transfer was authorised.”

The woman turned toward Noah.

He shook his head. “I wasn't at the medical station.”

Elias looked down.

The woman saw it. “You were.”

“I was in the corridor,” he said. “I heard the order.”

“Whose order?”

He did not answer.

The terminal chimed. A new record had arrived from Saint Oran's. It contained a voice message, short and distorted.

A man spoke.

“Elias, if you are hearing this, you should know that I remember the original. I remember the woman who died. I remember the woman who walked out. They were both real.”

The message ended.

Elias closed his eyes.

“Who was that?” the woman asked.

He opened them again. “The person who authorised the transfer.”

“Name.”

“Director Halden.”

Noah looked at him with sudden anger. “Halden died in the first week.”

“That's what the records say,” Elias replied.

Outside the gate, Ruth raised her hand. The infected began to move again, slowly turning toward the street.

The Auditor's terminal displayed a final line:

WITNESS REQUIRED. LOCATION: SAINT ORAN'S. WINDOW: 36 HOURS.

The woman looked at the people gathered in the common room: the doctor who had signed a death record, the engineer who had helped build the network, the scientist who had failed to stop a trial, the woman who had chosen her own name, and the people who had begun to build a settlement without knowing what they were building.

“Then we go to Saint Oran's,” she said.

Sera shook her head. “We just got back.”

“I know.”

“We have a sick child, a new patient, a generator that still needs watching, and a perimeter full of infected.”

“I know.”

“And now you want to leave because a stranger brought us a photograph.”

The woman looked at the three contradictory records.

“No,” she said. “I want to leave because someone has made three different versions of my death, and one of them is being used to decide who I am.”

Sera held her gaze. Then she nodded once.

“Fine. We go prepared.”

Ruth closed the white case.

“There's one more thing,” she said.

The woman waited.

“The Auditor has a rule. No witness travels alone. If you come to Saint Oran's, bring someone who remembers you before the event.”

The woman looked around the room.

For a moment, nobody volunteered.

Then Elias stepped forward.

“I remember,” he said.
