THE MERIDIAN OF ASH · BOOK I · CHAPTER 21

CHAPTER TWENTY-ONE — TWO WOMEN, ONE KEY

THE OUTBREAK · ENGLISH ORIGINAL

For a moment, the server room contained two versions of the same silence.

The woman in the doorway had Lin's face, but not quite her stillness. She held her shoulders differently. Her weight rested on the balls of her feet, ready to move. A faint scar crossed the underside of her chin, and her hair was longer, tucked behind one ear. The magenta ring beneath her wrist was brighter than Lin's, almost white at its centre.

Lin took one step backward.

“Mei,” she said.

The newcomer smiled. “You remember.”

Sera raised her rifle. “Stay where you are.”

Mei looked at the weapon with mild interest. “You have no idea what you're pointing at.”

“It's a rifle. I've met them.”

“Then you know the difference between a threat and a warning.”

“I'm giving you both.”

The woman moved slightly between the two women. “Who is she?”

“My sister,” Lin said.

Mei's smile faded. “Half-sister. We were never very good at family labels.”

“Are you human?” Noah asked.

Mei looked at him. “That's a rather personal question.”

“Answer it.”

“I was born. I grew up. I made mistakes. I have a pulse. Is that enough for you?”

“Not anymore,” Noah said.

The console continued to hum. The remote copy request had frozen at thirty percent, but the connection remained open. Jun was watching the status panel, afraid to touch anything.

Lin's device flashed again.

THIRD SIGNATURE: PRESENT. REUNIFICATION: AVAILABLE.

Mei stepped into the room.

“Don't let her near the console,” Lin said.

“I'm not here for the console.”

“Then why are you here?”

“To bring you home.”

Lin gave a short, bitter laugh. “You used that line before.”

Mei's gaze softened. “I was trying to save you.”

“You were trying to deliver me.”

“To people who could have kept you alive.”

“By turning me into a credential?”

The woman watched their faces. The resemblance was not only physical. They shared a habit of looking at a person as if they were measuring the distance between what was said and what was meant. But Lin's eyes held exhaustion. Mei's held certainty.

“Which one of you is the original?” the woman asked.

Both women looked at her.

Mei answered first. “I am.”

Lin said nothing.

The woman turned to her. “Is that true?”

“It's complicated.”

“That's not an answer.”

Lin glanced at the console. “Mei was born before me. We share a mother. Different fathers. She disappeared when I was nineteen.”

“Disappeared where?” Sera asked.

“Into a programme that did not officially exist.”

Mei tilted her head. “You make it sound like a kidnapping.”

“Wasn't it?”

“I volunteered.”

“You were twenty-two.”

“I was an adult.”

“You were recruited through a scholarship.”

“I was recruited because I was good.”

“And you came back with a new identity, a new medical record and a key that could open the Green Room.”

Mei's expression hardened. “You came back with a key too.”

The woman looked between them. “Both of you were custodians?”

“Not at first,” Lin said. “Mei was part of a field-verification unit. I was a courier. We were assigned to different parts of the same programme.”

“Why do you have the same face?”

Mei answered. “Because we are sisters.”

“Not the same face,” the woman said. “The same biometric signature.”

Noah moved toward the console. “That is the question.”

He brought up the two profiles. The system displayed matching facial geometry, similar voice patterns, and a high degree of overlap in their biological markers. But the match was not complete. There were differences in bone structure, immune response and neural activity.

“Close relatives,” Noah said. “Not duplicates.”

Mei smiled. “Thank you, doctor.”

“But the wrist profiles are almost identical,” he continued. “That isn't genetic. Someone copied the access pattern.”

Lin looked at her sister. “You gave them your body.”

Mei's face changed. “I gave them a sample.”

“Without telling me.”

“You were not part of the project.”

“I was your sister.”

“And you were the reason I joined.”

The words hung between them.

The woman felt the old room in the laboratory return: the glass wall, the table, the voice telling her that the test was not a test. She understood that this was how secrets multiplied. One person believed they were protecting another. One person accepted a bargain. A third person used the bargain as proof that consent had been given.

“Who copied the wrist profile?” she asked.

Mei looked at the woman. “You did.”

The answer was so unexpected that nobody spoke.

“You signed a biometric access protocol before the outbreak,” Mei continued. “Your profile was used to test the emergency identity system. Lin's was derived from mine. Mine was derived from the first field custodian. We were all connected through the same access architecture.”

“Who was the first field custodian?”

Mei looked at the ceiling.

“Your mother,” she said.

The woman's breath caught.

Noah turned sharply. Elias stepped toward her, but she raised one hand to stop him.

“My family history is not in your files,” she said.

“Not in the files you have,” Mei replied.

“Don't use that word. Files.”

Mei nodded once. “Fair.”

The woman stared at her. “You don't know who my mother was.”

“I know the identity that was used to create the first custodian profile. I don't know whether it was her real identity.”

“Then you know nothing.”

“I know that the first access credential was signed by a woman who used your family name. I know the signature was accepted. I know the person who issued it was already dead according to the civil registry.”

The room seemed to tilt. The woman could feel the serum vial against her ribs, the sealed glass pressing through the fabric.

“Why didn't you tell me at Saint Oran's?”

“Because I wanted to know whether you would come here.”

“You used me.”

“Yes.”

Lin stepped forward. “Mei, stop.”

“No. She deserves the truth.”

“Then tell her why you came.”

Mei looked at the console. “Because the checkpoint is incomplete. It needs both field signatures to unlock the original training record.”

“Your signature and Lin's?”

“And hers.”

The woman shook her head. “I am not giving you anything.”

“You already have.”

The console chimed.

A new window appeared, showing a transfer package that had been waiting in a local cache. Its metadata contained three signatures, not four. One belonged to Lin. One belonged to Mei. The third was the woman's.

The fourth field was blank.

CUSTODIAN FOUR: UNRESOLVED.

“Who is the fourth?” Elias asked.

Mei looked at him.

“You know,” she said.

Elias went pale.

The woman turned. “Tell me.”

He looked at the two women, then at the console.

“Mira,” he said. “Mira was the fourth.”

Mei nodded. “And she is still alive.”

The server room lights dimmed. The remote connection resumed, advancing from thirty percent to thirty-one.

On the console, a new message appeared.

MIRA VENN REQUESTING CONTACT.

A voice followed, quiet and distorted.

“Elias. Do not let them reunite the sisters.”

Mei closed her eyes.

Lin whispered, “She's alive.”

The voice continued.

“And do not trust the woman who came with you.”

The woman looked at Lin.

Lin had brought her to the Green Room. Mei had arrived only moments earlier, wearing Lin's face. The warning could point to either woman—or be designed to make the woman doubt them both.

Then at Mei.

Then at the black device on the console.

For the first time, she understood that the two sisters might not be the only people in the room who had been copied.
