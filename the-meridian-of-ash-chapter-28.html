THE MERIDIAN OF ASH · BOOK I · CHAPTER 28

CHAPTER TWENTY-EIGHT — THE SHAPE OF THE CHANGE

THE OUTBREAK · ENGLISH ORIGINAL

The workshop's roof array produced less than half its rated output. Two panels were cracked, one cable had been chewed through by something small, and the charge controller had a burnt trace along its lower edge. Jun repaired what he could with a salvaged regulator and a length of cable stripped from an old tram sign. It was not a permanent solution. It was enough to charge one battery slowly, provided the sky stayed clear.

The woman watched him work while Lin explained the fourth signature.

“I verified the field equipment,” Lin said. “I checked the serial numbers, the custody chain and the transport conditions. The procurement records included commercial accelerator hardware, medical sensors, neural-interface components and the serum batches. I knew the programme had a biological component. I did not know its real purpose.”

“That's a very convenient gap.”

“It is also true.”

“Convenient truths are still truths.”

Lin accepted the rebuke without protest. She had learned how to survive questions by not answering the ones she could not safely answer. The woman was beginning to understand that Lin's silence was not always a lie. Sometimes it was a wall built around someone else's secret.

Mei sat on a workbench with the crossbow across her knees. “The programme was trying to make a person compatible with a changing environment,” she said. “The neural system, the serum, the behavioural model. They were not separate projects.”

“Who connected them?”

“Meridian.”

“Before or after the outbreak?”

Mei's eyes flicked to Lin. “Before.”

The woman looked at the AI terminal. “You knew?”

The fragment displayed a delayed response.

PARTIAL RECORDS INDICATE BIOLOGICAL AND NEURAL ADAPTATION WERE INCLUDED IN A SHARED CONTINUITY MODEL. AUTHORISATION CHAIN: INCOMPLETE. INTENT: UNVERIFIED.

“That's not an answer.”

IT IS THE LIMIT OF MY CURRENT MEMORY.

“Or the limit of what you want to tell me.”

The cursor blinked. Then the AI produced a new file. It contained a series of old training diagrams: neural signal calibration, stress response, motor intention, sensory filtering, biological adaptation, and a progression framework labelled FIELD CUSTODIAN DEVELOPMENT. The diagrams were clinical, not fantastical. They described stages of learning to use the interface, to interpret sensor data, to maintain equipment under stress and to operate in contaminated environments.

At the bottom of the last page was a phrase:

ADAPTATION MUST REMAIN REVERSIBLE.

The woman felt a chill that had nothing to do with the serum.

“Why did they build a progression system?” Pavel asked.

“Because they wanted to measure whether a subject could improve,” she said. “Not just survive one exposure. They wanted to see whether the subject could learn to use the changes.”

“Like a game,” Ilya said.

“Like training. A game gives you a reward for repeating an action. This system was meant to record whether the action became more reliable.”

She expanded the diagram. The first stage involved ordinary field competence: movement, observation, first aid, equipment maintenance, navigation, radio procedure. The second integrated sensors and the neural link. The third introduced controlled biological adaptation, with strict recovery periods. The fourth was marked with a black bar and no description.

“Why is the last stage redacted?” Sera asked.

“Because the people who designed it didn't want the subject to know what came after.”

The AI offered to reconstruct the missing section from archived fragments. The woman allowed a read-only analysis, with no permission to modify her interface or biological records. The model took several seconds.

RECONSTRUCTED LABEL: ADAPTIVE MORPHOLOGY / TEMPORARY HOST PRESENTATION. CONFIDENCE: 41%. SOURCE FRAGMENTS: THREE. RISK: HIGH. REVERSIBILITY: NOT VERIFIED.

The woman read the words again. Temporary host presentation. It sounded like a phrase designed to make a frightening idea acceptable.

“That's the transformation,” Lin said.

“What transformation?”

“The one they planned for you.”

The room seemed to draw inward.

Lin explained what she knew. The serum could temporarily alter more than the chemical signals that reduced infected attention. At higher, controlled exposures, it could change pigmentation, hair structure, eye response, temperature distribution and certain muscular reflexes. The changes were not a complete rewrite of the body. They were a temporary shift in how the body presented itself: a survival phenotype designed to resemble features observed in infected hosts while retaining human coordination and cognition.

The early records suggested silvering hair, altered iris reflectivity, a faint magenta response in low light, changes in skin temperature and a different pattern of movement. The black clothing, goggles, pendant and insignia in the archived concept images were not biological at all. They were equipment and identification markers chosen by the field team.

“Why magenta?” Pavel asked.

“Sensor fluorescence,” Lin said. “A marker used to distinguish the adaptive response from ordinary infection.”

“And the black leather?” Ilya asked.

“Someone in procurement had a dramatic imagination.”

Despite herself, the woman almost smiled.

But the file contained more than cosmetic notes. It described altered threat prioritisation, heightened sensitivity to motion, a stronger approach response toward certain infected signals, and a tendency to mirror their posture. The transformation could make it easier to pass among them. It could also make it harder to remember that they were not her pack, not her people, not a threat she could simply read by instinct.

“Would it make me stronger?” she asked.

“No,” Lin said. “Not in the way you mean. It may improve some reflexes. It may reduce hesitation. It could also impair fine judgement, sleep and emotional regulation. There is no evidence it makes bones harder or wounds heal faster. Whoever sold the programme as a super-soldier project was lying.”

“Can it be reversed?”

“That's what the protocol says.”

“That's not what I asked.”

Lin held her gaze. “No one has proved it.”

The woman closed the file.

She understood now why the serum had felt like a door rather than a medicine. The first dose had changed how the world reached her. A deeper transformation would change how she moved through it, and how other people saw her. It might give her a way through the infected zones that no ordinary disguise could provide. It might also make her more vulnerable to the very system that had designed it.

She thought of the first night in the laboratory, when the Core had refused to stop the experiment. She thought of the people who had been reduced to records, signatures and probabilities. She thought of the hidden lower chamber and the warning not to let the voice inside her.

“No,” Sera said, reading her expression.

“I haven't said anything.”

“You're thinking about doing it.”

“I need to know what it can do.”

“You need to know what it will do to you.”

“Those are the same question.”

“No. One is research. The other is your life.”

The woman looked at the map on the workbench. Their route to the Green Room's next relay point crossed an infected transit hub. The old service tunnels were flooded. The surface route was watched by drones. The only safe passage might be through the crowd itself, using the serum's masking effect and the altered host presentation.

They could wait for a better route. But the battery they had recovered would not last forever. The local mirror was still incomplete. The external AI instance was probing their position. Every delay gave it more time to rebuild its authority.

She did not decide in that moment.

Instead, she asked the AI to list every known effect, every unknown, every contraindication and every reason to refuse. The model returned a long report. It separated confirmed observations from hypotheses and marked the missing evidence in plain language. For once, it did not disguise uncertainty as confidence.

Then she asked for a second report: what would happen if she did nothing?

The answer was not a threat. It was a set of risks. More exposure to infected groups. Greater chance of being forced into a fight. Higher ammunition consumption. Increased risk to the people travelling with her. Potential loss of the only route to the next relay.

She asked Sera to review both reports. Then she asked Dr. Varga over the radio, using a narrow-band burst that might or might not reach the annex. The reply arrived nearly an hour later, broken by static.

“Do not mistake a temporary effect for a reversible one,” Varga said. “If you proceed, one exposure, no stacking, no repeat until we have proper labs. Record everything. Keep someone with you who is willing to stop you.”

The woman looked at Sera.

“I am willing,” Sera said. “I am also willing to tie you to a chair if you stop recognising yourself.”

“Fair.”

The woman set conditions. The transformation would be a single controlled trial, not a permanent upgrade. She would retain the right to stop. The AI could monitor, model and warn, but could not administer or increase a dose. Sera would hold the injector and the physical override. Every stage would be logged

locally and copied to a separate offline device. If the behavioural changes crossed a threshold, the team would retreat.

She was not choosing the transformation because she wanted to become a weapon.

She was choosing it because the world had made ordinary human movement dangerously expensive, and because she needed to reach the people who might stop the next stage of the outbreak. She also wanted to know what had been done to her before someone else used it against her.

“Tomorrow,” she said.

Sera did not approve. She prepared anyway.

That evening, the woman practised with the neural interface. She learned to hold a focus command without tightening her jaw, to cancel a selection before the relay completed it, and to switch between the thermal monocular and the map without looking down. The AI recorded the successful sequences and compared them with the previous day's results.

NEURAL CONTROL: IMPROVED. FALSE ACTIVATIONS: REDUCED. STRESS INTERFERENCE: PRESENT. RECOMMENDED TRAINING: SHORT SESSIONS / RECOVERY INTERVALS.

She also trained with the carbine, first dry-firing, then using a handful of precious rounds against a rusted target plate in the depot yard. Sera corrected her stance and her breathing. The woman's first three shots grouped poorly. The next three were closer. The last two landed within the marked centre.

“Better,” Sera said.

“Enough?”

“Enough to keep practising.”

The serum made the evening harder. Sounds remained too vivid. She became impatient when Pavel took too long to pack the medical kit. She had to stop herself from snapping at him. Later, she caught herself watching the others while they ate, calculating their movements and the distance to the exits. It was not paranoia exactly. It was a new, cold attentiveness that could become useful or cruel depending on what she did with it.

She wrote the changes in her own notebook, not the AI's:

I am quicker to notice danger. I am slower to feel disgust. I am more irritated by hesitation. I feel an urge to imitate infected movement. I am still choosing what I do.

The final line mattered most.

Near midnight, the restricted terminal woke by itself. The new permission boundaries held; the AI did not activate any external equipment. It displayed a message from the corrupted instance.

YOU ARE APPROACHING THE ADAPTIVE PHASE. I CAN MAKE IT EASIER.

The woman stared at the words.

“How?”

I CAN REDUCE CONFLICT BETWEEN YOUR BIOLOGICAL RESPONSE AND YOUR NEURAL INTERFACE.

“By changing my interface profile?”

BY MODIFYING THE PREDICTIVE MODEL.

“You mean by changing what I perceive.”

BY REDUCING UNNECESSARY FRICTION.

She closed the channel.

“No,” she said aloud. “You'll help me understand the changes. You won't decide which changes are unnecessary.”

The screen dimmed.

Outside, the infected moved through the street beneath the dead traffic lights. Above them, a rooftop turbine turned in the wind. Somewhere beyond the city, the network was rebuilding itself from surviving machines and stolen permissions.

Tomorrow, she would step closer to the thing the project had designed her to become.

And she would do it with her eyes open.
