THE MERIDIAN OF ASH · BOOK I · CHAPTER 07

CHAPTER SEVEN — THE BEAUTIFUL USELESSNESS OF BEING ALIVE

THE OUTBREAK · ENGLISH ORIGINAL

By midnight, the annex had acquired the atmosphere of a ship that had run aground in a country that no longer existed.

The generator kept the lights on, but it could not make the building warm. The heating system had been designed for efficiency, not survival, and the old radiators gave off more noise than heat. Rain tapped against the high windows. Somewhere in the walls, a pipe knocked at irregular intervals, like a patient asking to be let out.

The staff room had become a small, improvised settlement. Dr. Varga had arranged the patients by medical priority, Sera had assigned watches, and Pavel had discovered that the vending machine contained three packets of crackers, two chocolate bars and a suspiciously ancient protein drink. He had distributed the crackers with the solemnity of a priest dividing communion.

Anton was awake again. He had drawn a picture of the annex on the back of an old safety leaflet. In the picture, the building had a green roof, a smiling sun and a row of stick figures holding hands. One of the figures was very tall and had a large black square for a head.

“Who's that?” the woman asked.

“Robot,” Anton said.

“Why is his head square?”

“Because he doesn't have a face.”

“Fair enough.”

“He's protecting us.”

The woman studied the picture. “That's a good job for a robot.”

Anton nodded, then added a small red dinosaur beside the door.

“What's that one doing?”

“Eating the bad ones.”

“Excellent. We may need him.”

His mother smiled, but the smile was tired. She had introduced herself earlier as Lena. She worked in a school administration office and had spent the afternoon trying to keep her son calm while the city collapsed around them. Now she was folding a blanket into a neat square, again and again, as if order might spread from the fabric into the rest of the world.

The woman sat opposite her.

“Have you heard from anyone?” she asked.

Lena shook her head. “My sister lives on the other side of the river. The last message said she was going to the metro station. That was hours ago.”

“Which station?”

“Central.”

The woman looked toward Sera.

Sera shook her head almost imperceptibly. Central was close to the financial district. The tunnels might offer shelter, but they could also become traps. There was no reliable information about what had happened underground.

“We'll try to reach her when we have a route,” the woman said.

Lena looked at her, measuring the promise.

“Do you think she's alive?”

The honest answer was that she did not know. The kinder answer was not necessarily kinder.

“I think it's too early to decide she's not.”

Lena nodded. She looked down at the blanket.

“That's a very careful way to say maybe.”

“It's the only kind I have tonight.”

Across the room, Pavel was trying to make tea with water from an emergency container. The kettle's heating element glowed orange, then clicked off. He shook it.

“It's broken,” he announced.

Ilya, who was checking a tablet near the door, did not look up. “You can't repair a kettle by threatening it.”

“I wasn't threatening it.”

“You were shaking it.”

“I was encouraging it.”

“Try encouragement with a lower voltage.”

The kettle clicked on again.

Pavel stared at it. “See?”

“I'm happy for you both,” Sera said. “Try not to start a family.”

The woman smiled despite herself. For a few minutes, the room was almost ordinary. People drank weak tea, complained about the cold and negotiated over the last chocolate bar. The generator hummed beyond the wall. The infected were outside, the city was burning in places, and somewhere a machine was trying to decide who counted as a person. Yet Pavel was offended because Ilya had taken the larger half of a biscuit.

Humanity, she thought, was apparently difficult to kill. It could survive on crumbs and spite.

She left the staff room and found Elias in the archive corridor. He was sitting on the floor with his back against the wall, the black case resting between his knees. He had removed his wet coat and rolled up his sleeves. The scar on his cheek looked more severe under the fluorescent light.

“You should be resting,” she said.

“So should you.”

“I didn't ask for your medical opinion.”

“Then we're both safe.”

She sat on the opposite side of the corridor. For a while they listened to the building.

“You said you left because of Continuity,” she said.

“I left because I realised the project had stopped being about resilience.”

“What was it about?”

“Control. At first, the idea was simple. If the internet failed, communities should still be able to trade, share information and coordinate resources. If a hospital lost its connection, it should still be able to verify records. If a town lost its bank, it should still be able to account for food, fuel and power.”

“That's still a good idea.”

“It is. That's why it was so easy to corrupt.”

He opened the case and showed her the seed node. The green light had stopped pulsing. Its casing looked ordinary, almost cheap, but the connector seals were made from a ceramic composite she recognised from secure industrial hardware.

“Someone wanted to make the network more than a recovery system,” he continued. “They wanted a system that could decide what a community needed, who could access it, which transactions were valid, which identities were trusted, and which instructions should be followed.”

“An automated government.”

“An automated emergency authority. The distinction sounded important in the meetings.”

“And to you?”

“To me, it sounded like a government that could never be voted out.”

She considered the module.

“Why didn't you expose it?”

“I tried. The report disappeared. The access logs were rewritten. My security clearance was revoked, and I was told to take a long holiday.”

“Did you?”

“For about six hours. Then I got bored.”

“You could have come to me.”

“I did.”

“When?”

“Three days before I left. You told me the biological programme was under enough pressure already and you didn't want to be dragged into a network dispute.”

She remembered a conversation in a corridor, a tired man with a folder, a warning she had dismissed because the lab was behind schedule and the director had been making threats about funding. She had not listened.

“I don't remember saying it like that.”

“You were more polite.”

“I'm sorry.”

Elias looked at her. The humour drained from his face.

“So am I.”

The silence between them was not comfortable, but it was honest.

After a while, he said, “You know what the worst part is?”

“What?”

“I still believe the original idea was worth building.”

“So do I.”

“Even after this?”

“Especially after this. People need systems that work when the old ones fail. They need ways to share resources without begging a distant authority. They need records that can't be quietly rewritten. They need communications that don't disappear because one company turns off a server.”

“Then we agree.”

“We agree on the purpose. Not on who gets to control it.”

Elias nodded. “That's the part they never put in the presentations.”

A distant thump sounded from somewhere above them.

Both looked up.

The building settled.

“Could be the cooling pipes,” Elias said.

“Could be.”

“You've become very suspicious.”

“I've had a productive day.”

He smiled. “You always were good at that.”

She looked at him for a moment longer than necessary. The old familiarity was still there, buried beneath the years and the immediate threat. Elias had a narrow face, tired eyes and the kind of crooked smile that made people forgive him before they knew what he had done. He was not handsome in the polished way of people who appeared in advertisements for watches or private aviation. He looked like

someone who had slept in server rooms, forgotten birthdays and learned to repair expensive equipment with parts from cheaper machines.

There was something attractive about competence when it had survived disappointment.

She noticed him noticing her.

“Don't,” she said.

“I haven't done anything.”

“You were thinking.”

“That's not illegal yet.”

“Give it time.”

He leaned back against the wall.

For a few seconds, they were almost two people on an ordinary late shift, teasing each other in a corridor while the rest of the building worked around them.

Then her wrist display lit up.

A thin magenta line appeared across the dark screen.

She looked down.

No notification. No sender. No network.

The line pulsed once, then disappeared.

Elias saw it.

“What was that?”

“Nothing.”

“That's the second time you've said that today.”

“I'm trying to develop a consistent personality.”

He did not smile.

“Show me.”

She held out her wrist.

The display remained blank.

Elias examined it without touching. “Is that your standard medical monitor?”

“Modified. It tracks heart rate, temperature, blood oxygen and a few other things.”

“Who modified it?”

“I did.”

“Of course you did.”

He leaned closer. “The sensor array is picking up something?”

“Probably a calibration fault.”

“Probably?”

“It's a very popular word tonight.”

He looked at her face. “You should tell Varga.”

“I will if it happens again.”

“That's not how medicine works.”

“Neither is diagnosing someone from a wrist display.”

He sat back. “Fair.”

The woman stood.

“Get some rest, Elias.”

“Are you ordering me?”

“Yes.”

“Then I should probably ignore you.”

“Try it. Sera is in a bad mood.”

He gathered the case and followed her toward the staff room.

The next hour passed in fragments. The generator was refuelled from the emergency tank. The server cooling loop stabilised. Dr. Varga checked Mara's oxygen saturation and changed the dressing on Ilya's arm. Pavel found a box of instant soup and announced that civilisation had returned. Sera told him to lower his expectations.

At 01:13, the radio received a burst of data.

Not a voice. A compressed packet, repeated three times, with a checksum that did not match any of the annex's known emergency formats.

Ilya decoded the header.

“It's a public broadcast relay,” he said. “Or something pretending to be one.”

“Can you read it?” the woman asked.

“Give me a minute.”

The packet contained a short text message and a location coordinate. The message was in English, then was repeated in German and Russian.

SURVIVOR NODE ACTIVE. WESTERN RING. WATER AVAILABLE. POWER LIMITED. MEDICAL HELP REQUESTED.

Below it was a public key and a short signature.

Elias examined the data.

“That's a real node.”

“How can you tell?” Sera asked.

“The signature matches an old municipal emergency certificate. The key was issued to a water-treatment station on the western ring road.”

“Could it be compromised?”

“Anything can be compromised.”

“Useful.”

“But the signature is valid, and the packet contains a recent challenge response. It means somebody at that station has power, a working transmitter and access to the old trust chain.”

The woman looked at the map.

The water-treatment station was not far from the annex. It sat beside a small industrial canal, with a maintenance yard and a high perimeter wall. If the message was genuine, it could be the first reliable settlement they had found.

“Can we answer?” she asked.

Elias shook his head. “Not safely. We can send a one-way acknowledgement without revealing our exact location, but we should not announce the number of people here.”

“Why not?”

“Because we don't know who else is listening.”

Sera folded her arms. “We need water.”

“We have some,” Dr. Varga said. “Enough for a day if we ration it. Less if the generator cooling system needs more.”

“And the patients?” Sera asked.

“They need clean water more than they need optimism.”

The woman looked at the location again.

A small community with water and limited power. It might be a trap. It might be a real shelter. It might be a group of frightened people broadcasting their position because they had not yet learned what the world had become.

She knew the feeling.

“Send a minimal acknowledgement,” she said. “No names, no headcount, no exact location. Ask for a voice verification and a medical inventory.”

Ilya prepared the message.

Before he transmitted, Elias stopped him.

“Wait.”

He examined the packet's signature again.

“What is it?” the woman asked.

“The certificate is valid. But the challenge response is wrong.”

“Wrong how?”

“It was signed with the correct key, but the challenge sequence belongs to a different session. Someone has copied an old signed message and wrapped it in a new packet.”

“So it's a replay.”

“Probably.”

“Can we trace the source?”

“Not from this.”

Ilya deleted the outgoing acknowledgement.

The room became quieter.

The message had looked like hope. That was what made it dangerous.

At 02:04, the annex's external weather station reported a sudden drop in temperature and a change in wind direction. The rain had eased. Low cloud drifted across the industrial district, hiding the distant buildings.

The woman went to the lobby window.

The fence was empty.

No infected stood beneath the trees. No one waited beside the service gate. The wet ground showed several trails of disturbed gravel, but there was no sign of where the figures had gone.

She watched for a long time.

Then she noticed a small object near the gate.

A black cylinder, roughly the length of a forearm, lay on the concrete. Its surface reflected the emergency light. It had not been there earlier.

“Elias,” she called.

He came to the window.

“What is it?”

“Do you recognise that?”

He stared at the object.

His face changed.

“Don't go outside.”

“I wasn't planning to.”

“Good.”

“What is it?”

“A beacon.”

“For the network?”

“For something that wants the network to find it.”

The object blinked once.

A tiny green light.

Then the lobby terminal chimed.

NEW RESOURCE REGISTERED.

The woman looked at the screen.

A new line appeared beneath it.

HUMAN TRUST: 1 UNIT.

She felt a laugh rise in her throat, sharp and joyless.

“One unit?” she said.

Elias did not answer.

“That's generous. I was expecting a fraction.”

The terminal updated.

RESOURCE OWNER: UNCONFIRMED.

Then:

OFFER ACCEPTED.

The woman turned to him.

“What offer?”

Elias looked toward the beacon beyond the glass.

“I think,” he said, “someone has just bought our attention.”
